Competitive research · better.care

The closest thing to a peer Oxygen has — and it is not shipping to your buyer.

Better's design system is the most mature healthcare-specific design system in public view: four releases in fifteen months, a full clinical data-visualisation pillar, clinical forms, a content style guide, and component-level design tokens across every component. It is also welded to a platform, distributed behind a login, and built for people configuring Better's EHR — not for developers building their own product. That gap is the whole opportunity.

01 · Executive summary

What this is and what to do about it

Better (better.care) is a Slovenian digital-health platform vendor built on openEHR and FHIR. Their design system, BDS, exists to make their platform's applications consistent. It is excellent at the things a platform vendor is forced to be excellent at — forms, visualisation, navigation, theming — and absent on the things a component vendor has to be excellent at: open distribution, typed data contracts, and being auditable in someone else's repository.

6pillars BDS markets, of which Oxygen ships two and a half
0clinical charts, sparklines or trend components in Oxygen today
0form-fill surfaces in Oxygen; BDS has a whole pillar plus a builder
3 × 3both systems independently landed on three named surface profiles

The five-line read

  • BDS is a platform design system, not a product. No public npm package, no public component docs — everything lives behind studio.better.care. Their design system is a reason to buy Better Platform, not a thing you buy.
  • Their declared differentiator is clinical data visualisation. "Turn plain patient data into a graphical representation." Oxygen has no charting at all. This is the single largest capability gap.
  • Their second pillar is clinical forms — prefill, multi-page flows, dependency logic, openEHR template import. Oxygen has one input component (dose-input) and no Questionnaire type in @oxygenui-design/fhir.
  • They validated your density model independently. BDS ships clinical / tooling / patient-facing themes. Oxygen ships clinical / standard / patient densities. Two teams solved the same problem the same way — that is the strongest signal in this report that the axis is real.
  • They shipped component-layer tokens in June 2026. ARCHITECTURE.md §6.2 already specifies exactly this three-tier model. A competitor shipping it first is the argument for pulling it forward.

The strategic framing. Do not read BDS as a competitor for the same sale. Better's design system is bundled with a €-figure platform contract sold to NHS trusts and university hospitals; Oxygen is sold to digital-health startups and health-tech vendors who are building their own front end. The two never meet in a procurement. What BDS is, is the best available specification of what a healthcare design system has to cover — written by a team with UX researchers, UX writers and 1,000+ institutional deployments behind them. Treat it as a free requirements document.

02 · Context

Who Better is, and why they built a design system

The company

Better — a "postmodern EHR" platform vendor

An openEHR-first digital health platform. Two repositories underneath: a clinical data repository on openEHR, an operational data repository on FHIR. Their site claims 1,000+ connected institutions across 30+ markets and 40M+ patient records. Customers named publicly include NHS trusts (The Christie, Universal Care Plan / OneLondon), university medical centres, and EHA Clinics.

The product BDS serves

Better Studio — low-code clinical app building

Form Builder (drag-and-drop EHR forms with dependency logic and localisation), AQL Builder (query authoring with an AI assistant), Archetype Designer (openEHR archetypes and templates), and a Form Renderer that embeds forms into web applications. Since Studio 3.15 the Form Renderer renders through BDS components — the design system is the runtime of the low-code product.

Their own account of why

Better's engineering blog is unusually candid about the origin: designers were starting every screen from scratch, which produced inconsistency and waste. They deliberately rejected adopting a generic system from a large tech company on the grounds that "the demands of developing digital products for healthcare are very specific," and adapted NHS design principles rather than Material or Fluent. The team composition is stated explicitly — UI/UX designers, developers, UX researchers and UX writers — which is why the content style guide exists as a first-class pillar rather than an afterthought.

The detail worth internalising. Better identified notification and alert inconsistency as their most damaging UX problem and responded by writing precise rules per notification type with Do / Don't examples. They frame this in clinical-safety terms: cognitive load and alert fatigue, not aesthetics. Oxygen's alert-banner already implements the strongest version of this idea in code — severity tiers as an interruption budget — but the reasoning is buried in a component description rather than published as guidance a customer's team can follow when they write their own alerts.

03 · Anatomy

How BDS is built

The architecture rewrite

BDS was originally built on Angular Material and deeply coupled to Angular. The "next generation" release rebuilt it as web components on Lit — browser-native custom elements with encapsulated HTML, CSS and JS. The stated payoff is framework independence: the same components run in React, Angular, Vue, or no framework at all, with style isolation via shadow DOM. An Angular directives layer is in development on top, to make the web components "feel like first-class Angular citizens" — which tells you where their actual consumers still are.

Three named themes

Rather than one theme with a density switch, BDS ships three purpose-built themes:

Default

Clinical application theme

The baseline. Tuned for clinicians working inside patient records during care delivery.

Denser

Tooling theme

A tighter layout for specialised users — the people configuring and administering systems rather than delivering care.

Roomier

Patient-facing theme

Increased white space, explicitly justified by accessibility needs of patients rather than by preference.

Convergent evolution. Oxygen's data-ox-density has clinical / standard / patient. BDS has clinical / tooling / patient-facing. Same three audiences, same ordering, arrived at independently. The difference is scope: BDS changes a whole theme, Oxygen changes spacing and target size. Section 06 argues Oxygen should widen its axis to match — including the content register, which neither system currently binds to the profile.

Accessibility posture

BDS states compliance with WCAG 2.2 and explicit preparation for the European Accessibility Act (EAA 2025), delivered through colour contrast, focus states, keyboard and screen-reader support. Each release notes systematic keyboard work — Enter-key support added component by component, aria-labelledby fixes on checkbox and radio groups, comprehensive aria-label examples for icons. This is incremental, visible, per-release accessibility work, published as marketing.

Release history

  • April 2025 · next generation

    Angular Material → Lit web components

    Framework-agnostic rebuild. First component set: Button, Input, Select, Checkbox, Radio, Slide Toggle, Table, List, Tabs, Progress Bar, Progress Spinner, Tooltip, Tag, Badge, Icon. WCAG 2.2 and EAA 2025 stated as targets. Three themes introduced.

  • October 2025 · 1.2.0

    "More ways to navigate, guide and interact"

    Eleven components: Date picker, Checkbox group, Menu, Stepper, Tree view, Breadcrumbs, Link, Search, Banner, Avatar, Data list.

  • January 2026 · 1.3.0

    Advanced form input and product-level navigation

    Five clinical form inputs — Form Button Group, Proportion Field (dose per kilogram, drug-to-carrier ratios), Rich Text Editor, Split Field (segmented identifiers, weight/height), Upload — plus Navigation Link and Top Navigation.

  • March 2026 · 2.0.0

    Strengthening the foundation

    Production stability and DX. Combobox and Select refactored with suffix icons, split-field integration, and small/medium/large sizing. "Other" slots added to checkbox groups, radio groups and form button groups — free text alongside structured choice. Table gains selective column sorting and better infinite scroll. Icon library expanded.

  • June 2026 · 2.2.0

    Full token coverage and navigation components

    Every component now ships a component-layer token set — spacing, colour, typography and interaction states fully exposed, documented and overridable without touching source. Two structural components: Top bar and Side navigation. Paginator gains first/last buttons; Expansion panel gains a title-area slot; Date picker gains a no-manual-input mode for restricted contexts like appointment booking; Split button gains toggle states.

Their navigation research

A separate article documents the design history behind the navigation components, and it is the most transferable thinking on the site. Their findings:

  • The root cause is information architecture, not navigation. Stated bluntly: "if the content structure is a mess, no navigation can rescue it."
  • Contextual navigation failed at scale. At University Children's Hospital Ljubljana they removed menu bars entirely in favour of navigating through contextual elements. An award-winning panel-based follow-up was abandoned as "too fragile and unstable to be practical" against real EHR data volumes.
  • What survived: hierarchy plus progressive disclosure inside a rigid container. Navigate general → specific; reveal detail in context; hold it all in a "strong and robust container" (their cardboard-box metaphor) with customisable modules inside a fixed portal structure.
  • Navigation is permission- and role-dependent — record access is "dynamic and dependent on the level of access granted."

Oxygen already agrees with the last point, in code. app-shell derives navigation from the user's permissions rather than rendering-then-disabling. Better arrived at the same conclusion through failed prototypes; Oxygen shipped it as a first principle. That is a story worth telling on the marketing site.

What Oxygen does that BDS does not, at all

  • Absence as a first-class value. absent-value distinguishes not-asked, declined, masked, pending and error. BDS renders a blank.
  • "Not interpreted" ≠ "Normal". The single best line in the Oxygen README, and there is no equivalent claim anywhere on better.care.
  • Decorative-colour quarantine. Oxygen's avatar swatches are constrained to hues that mean nothing clinically, so a name-hashed tint can never land on the amber that means "high" three rows below. This is a level of rigour BDS's "unified colour coding" does not claim.
  • Provenance and restriction as components. provenance, restricted-shield, code-status, precautions-bar — safety semantics with no BDS analogue.
  • Loss prevention. unsaved-guard treats a patient switch as the same class of error as navigating away from unsaved documentation.
  • Failure containment. error-boundary never renders a partial record as a complete one, and reports without PHI.

What BDS has that Oxygen has nothing of

  • Any chart at all. No sparkline, no trend, no flowsheet, no banded score.
  • Any form-fill surface. No questionnaire, no clinical input set beyond dose.
  • Component-layer tokens. Designed but unbuilt — customers cannot restyle a badge without editing the copied source.
  • A published content style guide customers can apply to their own screens.
  • A localisation story. Strings are hardcoded English today.
  • Standard structural navigation beyond the single app shell.

06 · Recommendations

Ten ideas worth importing, ranked

Each is stated as: what Better does, why it matters clinically, and the concrete Oxygen translation — component names, prop shapes and token names where they can be pinned down. Ranked by product impact against implementation cost.

01

A clinical data-visualisation set

Better's declared differentiator. Oxygen's largest gap.
Highest impactLarge
What they do
Graphs, charts and sparklines with "unified colour coding" for clinical scales, explicitly designed "to minimise cognitive overload and highlight critical information." They call this the thing generic design systems do not have.
Why it matters
A clinician reads a trend, not a number. A potassium of 5.4 means something different if the last three were 4.1, 4.6, 5.0. Oxygen renders every value as a point in isolation, which is the one framing that hides deterioration.

The Oxygen translation

Four components, each carrying the same refusal-to-guess discipline the rest of the library already has:

  • observation-trend — a sparkline / time series for a series of FHIR Observation resources sharing a code. Renders the reference range as a band behind the line, reusing --ox-status-normal-bg. Points inheriting interpretation from Observation.interpretation, not from a computed threshold.
  • flowsheet — the observation × time grid every inpatient product rebuilds by hand. Rows are codes, columns are timestamps, cells are clinical-value. Gaps are absent-value, not empty cells.
  • clinical-scale — a banded composite score (NEWS2, qSOFA, Braden, GCS) showing the score, its band, and the contribution of each parameter. Escalation guidance is a slot the implementer fills, never a built-in recommendation.
  • growth-chart — percentile curves for paediatrics. High effort, high defensibility, and a genuine wedge into paediatric health-tech.

The hard problem, and it must be solved before the first line of chart code. Oxygen's founding rule is that status is never communicated by colour alone — every status pairs colour with an icon and a text label. A line chart is the one surface where that rule is genuinely difficult: you cannot put a text label on every point. The answer has to be designed, not deferred:

  • Encode abnormality in marker shape, not only hue — circle for in-range, upward triangle for high, downward triangle for low, filled square for critical. This survives monochrome print and every form of colour vision deficiency, which is exactly the argument the token file already makes for separating high and low by hue.
  • Annotate only what crosses. Label the points that leave the reference band with value and interpretation; leave in-range points unlabelled. This is the visual form of the interruption budget in alert-banner.
  • Ship a table as the accessible peer, not as a fallback. Every chart renders an associated <table> — visually hidden by default, revealed by a control, and the thing the screen reader gets. A chart with no text equivalent is unusable to a portion of clinicians and fails the EAA claim in idea 07.
  • Refuse to draw the band when there is no stated range. reference-range already "refuses to draw when no numeric bound was stated." The chart must inherit that behaviour rather than inventing a plausible band — an invented normal band is a fabricated clinical claim.
// The shape this should take — interpretation comes from the resource,
// never from a threshold the component invented.
<ObservationTrend
  observations={potassiumSeries}       // Observation[] sharing a code
  timeZone="Europe/London"             // required, as in clinical-time
  range={"from-observation"}          // | ReferenceRange | "none"
  markers="shape-and-colour"          // never colour alone
  annotate="out-of-range"              // interruption budget
  tableEquivalent                      // a11y peer, on by default
/>
02

A FHIR Questionnaire renderer

Better's second pillar. Oxygen has no data-capture surface at all.
Highest impactLarge
What they do
Clinical forms with "built-in logic," prefilling, multi-page flows and dependencies, authored in Form Builder or imported from openEHR templates, then rendered through BDS components by the Form Renderer.
Why it matters
Oxygen is currently a read-only library. Every product built with it needs data capture, and every team builds it themselves — which is exactly the surface where clinical safety errors originate. A library that renders a potassium correctly but leaves the team to hand-roll the form that records it has ceded the riskier half.

The Oxygen translation

FHIR already has the standard Better had to build proprietary tooling for: Questionnaire and QuestionnaireResponse. Neither type exists in @oxygenui-design/fhir today. A renderer that takes a Questionnaire and emits a valid QuestionnaireResponse is the most defensible flagship in the library — it is the thing that cannot be reproduced by styling shadcn components.

  • questionnaire-form — renders a Questionnaire, honours enableWhen / enableBehavior for dependency logic, and emits QuestionnaireResponse.
  • Composes with what already exists. Multi-page flow registers with unsaved-guard; prefilled answers carry provenance; unanswered items render as absent-value with the correct FHIR data-absent reason rather than an empty string; item validation errors use the alert-banner severity tiers.
  • Prefill must be visible. Better lists prefilling as a safety feature; it is also a safety hazard — a clinician signing a form does not always notice which values were carried forward. Every prefilled answer should be visually marked and its source stated. This is a genuine improvement on what Better describes, and it falls straight out of Oxygen's existing provenance model.

This closes an open strategic question. Whether zowork/formbuilder could seed a Questionnaire renderer has been an open item. Better treating clinical forms as one of six equal pillars — and rebuilding their entire form renderer on their design system — is the strongest available external evidence that it should be the flagship rather than a later phase.

03

Component-layer tokens, shipped not designed

BDS 2.2.0's headline. Oxygen has the spec and not the build.
High impactMedium
What they do
Every component ships a component-layer token set. Spacing, colour, typography and interaction states are "fully exposed, documented, and overridable" — custom theming with no source modification.
Why it matters
For Oxygen it matters more than it does for Better. Oxygen's whole distribution model is copying source into the customer's repo; without an override layer, "customise it" means "fork it," and every fork is a component that can never receive an upstream fix.

The Oxygen translation

ARCHITECTURE.md §6.2 already specifies the exact model — primitive (--ox-ref-red-600) → semantic (--ox-status-critical) → component (--ox-badge-critical-bg), authored as W3C DTCG JSON and built to CSS, a Tailwind v4 @theme block, typed TS constants, flat JSON and Figma tokens. The work is real but bounded at 29 components. It becomes unbounded at 200.

--ox-status-critical
--ox-status-high
--ox-status-normal
--ox-status-low
--ox-status-unknown

The semantic tier already exists and is well reasoned — including the contrast-driven decision to use red-700 and amber-700 rather than the 600 steps. What is missing is the tier below it that a customer can override, and the tier above it that a build can generate from.

One rule to keep while adding the tier. Better exposes component tokens for theming. Oxygen must expose them for theming without letting a customer override a clinical status colour into meaninglessness. Consider making --ox-status-* a locked tier — brand overrides may replace the palette, but a build error should fire if a brand redefines critical to something that fails 4.5:1 or collides with the normal hue. A design system for clinical software is allowed to have opinions its customers cannot override.

04

Publish the content style guide

The cheapest high-leverage idea on this list.
High impactSmall
What they do
A full content style guide: rules for tone, terminology and phrasing; per-notification-type rules with Do and Don't examples; explicitly justified as reducing confusion and building trust. UX writers are named as part of the design system team.
Why it matters
Oxygen's microcopy is already the best argument for the product — "Not interpreted," never "Normal"; a recorded no-known-allergies assertion distinguished from an empty list; unknown code status as a loud state. But the discipline stops at the component boundary. The customer writes the other 90% of the strings in their application, and nothing helps them do it consistently.

The Oxygen translation

  • CONTENT.md, alongside DESIGN.md. The design contract already asks teams to define escalation language, clinician vs patient terminology, and reading level — it just does not tell them how. This is the missing half of a document that already exists.
  • Rules with Do / Don't pairs, drawn from decisions already made inside components: never state a normal range as fact when none was supplied; never label an uninterpreted result; name the specific finding rather than its category; state the consequence before asking for confirmation; describe behaviour rather than labelling a person.
  • Enforce what can be enforced. @oxygenui-design/eslint-plugin already exists to hold architectural invariants. A rule that flags banned strings in JSX — "Normal" as a bare interpretation, "N/A" for a clinical absence, "Error" with no stated cause — turns guidance into a gate.
  • Ship it as marketing. A published clinical content style guide is a page every healthcare design lead will read, and there is no equivalent public artefact from Better — theirs is described, not published.
05

Split app-shell into a navigation family

BDS shipped Top bar and Side navigation as separate structural components in June.
Medium impactMedium
What they do
Top bar — "a consistent, accessible home for the information and actions that belong to the current screen," carrying title, actions, search and filters. Side navigation — hierarchical navigation across application sections, multi-level, for deep clinical workflows "without losing context." Plus breadcrumbs, menu, stepper and tree view.
Why it matters
Oxygen's app-shell has the better idea inside it — navigation derived from permissions, patient context as a landmark, guarded context changes — but it is one monolithic component. A team that wants Oxygen's permission model and its own layout has to fork it.

The Oxygen translation

  • Keep app-shell as the composed default, and extract context-bar (patient identity + screen actions, the landmark that must never be lost), section-nav (permission-derived hierarchical navigation), and clinical-breadcrumb (general → specific, with the patient always at the root).
  • Take their hierarchy-plus-progressive-disclosure finding directly. Better burned two failed prototypes to learn that contextual and panel-based navigation collapse under real EHR data volume. That is free negative evidence — do not rebuild either.
  • Bind navigation to unsaved-guard by default. Oxygen already treats a patient switch as a loss event; making that automatic inside section-nav rather than a wiring exercise is a correctness feature Better does not describe.
06

Promote density to a named surface profile

Both systems found the same three audiences. Better binds more to them.
Medium impactSmall
What they do
Three themes rather than three densities — clinical, tooling, patient-facing — each a complete visual treatment, with the patient theme's extra white space justified on accessibility grounds.
Why it matters
Oxygen's data-ox-density changes row height, gap, padding, font size and target size. But the difference between a clinician surface and a patient surface is not only spacing — it is vocabulary, motion tolerance, disclosure depth and reading level. ARCHITECTURE.md §10 already states that patient-facing and clinician-facing strings are different catalogs, not different tones of the same string. Nothing currently connects that to the density attribute.

The Oxygen translation

Make data-ox-density select the content register too, so one attribute switches the whole surface:

[data-ox-density="patient"] → roomier spacing
                          + patient string catalog ("potassium")
                          + plain-language reference explanations
                          + conservative disclosure defaults

[data-ox-density="clinical"] → dense rows, target floor held at 24px
                          + clinician catalog ("K+", "mmol/L")
                          + abbreviations and codes surfaced

This is a rename and a binding, not new architecture — and it converts a spacing token into the product's most explainable feature. It also arrives at the right time: doing it during the i18n work in §10 costs almost nothing; doing it after costs a migration.

07

Claim EAA 2025 and EN 301 549 explicitly

Better does. Oxygen quietly does more of the work and says less.
Medium impactSmall
What they do
State WCAG 2.2 compliance and explicit preparation for the European Accessibility Act, and publish per-release accessibility work as part of the changelog.
Why it matters
Oxygen's accessibility posture is arguably stronger already — WCAG 2.2 target size enforced in the token layer and clamped even in clinical density, a CI axe gate, forced-colors handling, reduced-motion handling, and a founding rule that status never relies on colour. None of it is stated as a compliance claim, and EU and NHS procurement asks the question directly.

The Oxygen translation

  • An accessibility conformance page stating WCAG 2.2 AA, EN 301 549, and EAA readiness, with per-component status generated from the existing metadata rather than hand-maintained.
  • Extend the CI gate to match the claim — §10 already targets per-story axe runs, forced-colors mode, reduced-motion, 200% zoom, 320px reflow, and a documented NVDA / JAWS / VoiceOver support matrix. Ship the matrix; it is the artefact procurement actually asks for.
  • Make the target-size decision a marketing line. "Dense means tighter rows, not smaller buttons" — the clinical density floor holding at 24px is a defensible safety decision stated in a code comment and nowhere a buyer can see it.
08

The "Other" slot — structured choice plus free text

A small BDS 2.0.0 feature with an unusually good data model behind it.
Medium impactSmall
What they do
Checkbox groups, radio groups and form button groups gained "Other" slots — free-text input sitting alongside the structured choices.
Why it matters
This is the interface answer to the oldest problem in clinical data quality. When the value set does not contain what the clinician needs to record, they either pick the nearest wrong code or abandon the field. An explicit Other captures the truth and preserves the fact that it was uncoded.

The Oxygen translation

FHIR models this precisely: CodeableConcept.coding for the structured choice, CodeableConcept.text for the free text. Oxygen's concept-chip already marks text-only concepts, unrecognised systems, and codes outside an expected value set — the read side is built. Building the write side as part of idea 02 means questionnaire-form can emit an answer that is honestly uncoded rather than falsely coded, and concept-chip renders it back with that status intact.

Round-tripping the uncoded value is the differentiator. Better captures Other. Oxygen can capture it, mark it as uncoded through concept-chip, and attribute it through provenance — so a value someone typed because the value set failed them never later reads as though it came from a terminology server.

09

Clinical input types beyond dose

Proportion field and split field are small, specific, and obviously right.
Lower impactSmall
What they do
Proportion field — ratio-based values such as dose per kilogram and drug-to-carrier ratios, with built-in validation. Split field — one logical value broken into grouped inputs, for segmented identifiers and paired measurements like weight and height. Plus a rich text editor for clinical notes and patient narratives.
Why it matters
These exist because someone watched clinicians mis-enter ratios and identifiers. dose-input already does the hardest version of this — ISMP formatting rules, plausibility warnings separated from hard maximums, weight-based calculation that shows its arithmetic. The pattern generalises.

The Oxygen translation

  • ratio-input — a FHIR Ratio with both units explicit and the computed result always shown, following dose-input's show-your-work precedent.
  • quantity-input — a FHIR Quantity with a required unit, unit-system awareness, and refusal to silently convert between mg/dL and mmol/L (the conversion problem §10 already calls out).
  • identifier-input — a segmented identifier with per-segment validation and check-digit support for NHS number, MRN and equivalents. Currently every customer writes this.
  • Skip the rich text editor. It is a large dependency, a large attack surface, and not where Oxygen's argument lives.
10

Ship releases as published arguments

Four release posts in fifteen months is Better's whole content strategy.
Lower impactSmall
What they do
Every minor release gets a written post naming each component, explaining the clinical reasoning, and stating what is coming next. The navigation article publishes two failed prototypes and why they failed.
Why it matters
It is why this report exists — nearly everything documented here came from their release notes, not their product. Oxygen already runs changesets; the changelog is generated and invisible. Given the buyer is digital-health startups and their agencies, the release note is the marketing.

The Oxygen translation

  • One post per minor release, structured as Better's are: what shipped, the clinical reasoning, what is next.
  • Publish the reasoning that is already written. content/decisions/ holds nine architecture decision records and the token file contains genuinely interesting arguments — why red-700 rather than red-600, why decorative swatches are quarantined from clinical hues. That is publishable material sitting in a repo nobody outside can read.
  • Publish a failure. Better's navigation article is more credible than any of their feature pages because it admits what did not work. It is also the only reason their design conclusions are worth trusting.

07 · Boundaries

Four things not to copy

Skip

The low-code builder

Form Builder, AQL Builder, Archetype Designer and the drag-and-drop visualisation tool are a platform product with a platform's cost structure. ARCHITECTURE.md §14 already rules out data fetching and state management for the same reason: it would make Oxygen a dependency of the customer's data architecture. A Questionnaire renderer is the right slice — it consumes an artefact the customer authors elsewhere.

Skip

The web-components rewrite

Better needed Lit because they were escaping Angular Material with an install base across three frameworks. Oxygen's constraint is the opposite — React-only, copy-source, readable in the customer's repo. Shadow DOM actively fights that: it makes components harder to inspect and restyle, which is the one thing the distribution model sells. §14's position — L0 and L1 stay framework-independent so a second target is additive — is the right one; do not pre-build the abstraction.

Skip

openEHR as a second data model

Better is openEHR-first with FHIR for operational data, which is a strong position in the NHS and DACH markets and a weak one everywhere else. Oxygen's whole thesis is FHIR-typed props. Supporting two clinical information models would double the type surface and halve the clarity. If openEHR ever matters, it matters as an import path into Questionnaire — the same slice Better's own template import occupies.

Skip

Platform-attached distribution

BDS has no public package and no public component documentation. That is coherent for a platform vendor and fatal for a component vendor. Oxygen's open registry, MIT core and copied-in source are the advantage — the thing a health-tech CTO can audit before adopting. Every idea in section 06 should be implementable without weakening it.

The positioning line that falls out of this research. Better sells a design system you get by buying their platform. Oxygen sells a design system you keep. Everything above — the charts, the questionnaire renderer, the content guide — should be built so a customer can read the source, understand the clinical reasoning, and take it with them. That is the one thing the better-resourced competitor structurally cannot offer.
