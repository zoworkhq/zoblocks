Section 01

The idea, sharpened

What you sketched is a good component. It is also, in its default form, the single most dangerous thing Oxygen could ship. Both of those are worth saying up front, because the second one is what makes the first one valuable.

The screenshots describe a floating dock with three task modes, slash shortcuts, dictation, and an expanding thread panel. That is a complete and coherent product idea, and your instinct about distribution is right: every clinical application being built in 2026 wants this surface, almost nobody wants to build it, and the parts that are hard to build are not the parts that look hard.

The parts that look hard are the floating positioning, the streaming text, the waveform. Those are two weeks of work. The parts that are actually hard are: deciding what the model is allowed to see, proving where an answer came from, keeping chart text from being read as instructions, and knowing — with evidence — whether the thing is making clinicians better or worse. Those are the parts a digital-health team cannot build in a sprint and will pay for.

The product thesis

Consult is not an AI chat component. It is the accountability layer around a model, delivered as a chat component. The chat is the part customers recognise; the provenance, scoping, audit and safety machinery is the part they are buying, whether or not they know it yet.

This reframing has a commercial consequence that matters more than the design one. An AI chat widget competes with a hundred free ones and with whatever the customer's frontend lead can assemble from Vercel's AI SDK in a fortnight. A component that arrives with a defensible answer to "how does your clinician independently review the basis for this recommendation?" competes with nothing, because that question has a regulatory shape (§11) and the free widgets do not answer it.

It also fits the buyer already identified in Oxygen's strategy — digital health startups, health-tech vendors, and their agencies. They have a model. They have an API key. What they do not have is six months of clinical-safety design, and they are the ones whose customers will ask.

What Consult must never be

Three constraints are load-bearing on every decision downstream. They are not hedges; they are the shape of the component.

  • Never autonomous. Consult proposes; a human commits. Every write to the record is attributed to the clinician who confirmed it, never to the model. This is a hard architectural rule in §9, not a policy statement.
  • Never unsourced. A clinical claim with no inspectable basis does not render as a clinical claim. It renders as an unsupported statement, visibly marked. §8.
  • Never the therapist. In behavioral health, three US states have already legislated on this and the answer is not subtle. §10.
Section 02

What to call it

Oxygen's naming system is "name the clinical instrument, then suffix the generic" — that is how rhythm-loader beat trace-loader and infusion-loader beat fill-loader. The same rule applied here produces an unusually good answer.

Recommendation

Consult. Registry item consult, npm @oxygenui-design/consult, component <Consult />, engine @oxygenui-design/consult-core.

The clinical instrument here is the curbside consult: the hallway question you ask a colleague who knows more than you about one thing. It is informal. It is advisory. It is not documented as an order. And — this is the part that makes the name do real work — the asking clinician remains the responsible party. That is not a metaphor for what this component does. It is a precise description, and it happens to be a near-verbatim statement of the FDA's fourth non-device criterion (§11).

A name that teaches the correct mental model to every developer who types it, and to every clinician who sees it in the UI, is worth more than a clever one. When someone proposes that Consult should file the order itself, the name argues back.

The shortlist, and why the others lost

CandidateForAgainst
consult chosen Exact clinical sense; encodes advisory-not-authoritative; reads well in behavioral health too, where "psychiatric consultation" is the collaborative care term. FHIR uses ServiceRequest.intent = consult for a referral. Mild collision, but in the same semantic family rather than a contradictory one.

Section 03

Anatomy

Your six screenshots are six states of two surfaces. Naming them precisely now is what keeps the API from growing a prop per screenshot later.

Surface A — the dock

Persistent, floating, always reachable. Its job is to accept a question in under two seconds and get out of the way. It has one collapsed form and three expansions.

  • 1Shortcut affordanceThe square glyph. Opens the slash menu; also the drop target for "/".
  • 2Question fieldSingle line, grows to four. "Ask anything…" is the wrong placeholder — see below.
  • 3Active mode chipShows the current mode. Opens the mode tray. Absent when no mode is set.
  • 4Dictation controlMic ⇄ live waveform. Two distinct states, never ambiguous.
  • 5SubmitAppears only when the field has content. Dark, high-contrast, unmistakable.
  • 6Scope stripNew. Not in your screenshots. The most important element on the surface — §8.
Surface B — the panel

Opens on first send. Your instinct to give it real estate on the left is right: the thread is a working document, not a notification.

  • 7Thread switcherTitle plus chevron. Threads are named from the question, editable, and persist.
  • 8Reasoning disclosureYour "Thought" label. Collapsed by default, and never confused with the answer.
  • 9Answer bodyStreamed. Inline citation markers, not a bibliography at the end.
  • 10Verify rowYour "Check other sources". Rename it. It is the criterion-4 control — §11.
  • 11Carry-out actionsCopy, insert into note, share. Each one is a provenance event.
  • 12Standing disclosureYour footer line. Keep it. Make it locale-aware and never scroll it away.

Three notes on what you already drew

"Ask anything…" is the one thing I would change on sight. It is a consumer chatbot's placeholder, and it makes a promise the component must not keep — that any question is in scope, including ones it should refuse. It also wastes the highest-value pixels on the surface. Replace it with the mode's actual affordance: "Ask about this patient…", "Search the evidence…", "What are you working through?" The placeholder should tell the clinician what this thing is for, and by implication what it is not for.

"Check other sources" is doing something more important than it sounds. Read literally it suggests the answer might be wrong and you should go look elsewhere — a vague nudge that people learn to ignore. Read as a product feature it is the control that lets a clinician inspect the basis of the recommendation, which is a regulatory requirement with a specific shape. Rename to "Show sources" and make it open a drawer containing the retrieved passages themselves, not a list of links. The difference between a link and the quoted passage is the difference between a citation and a verification.

Multi-select modes (your screenshot 4) is a subtle trap. Prepare + Work up active together reads as "more capable", but each mode is a scope contract — what data it may read, which tools it may call, what it may say (§7). Two modes at once means the union of two permission sets, and the union of two system prompts, which is a configuration nobody reviewed. Keep the modes single-select and let a shortcut span them if a real workflow needs it.

Section 04

The behavioural case, and the six laws it produces

This is the section that should determine the design, so it comes before the mockups rather than after them. Four findings from the human-factors literature bound what a floating clinical assistant is allowed to do.

+12.7% clinical errors per interruption during medication administration; major-error risk doubles from 2.3% to 4.7% at four interruptions Westbrook et al., Arch Intern Med 2010 — 98 nurses, 4,271 administrations
>90% override rate for drug–drug interaction alerts in contemporary EHRs; one study found 88.2% of very severe alerts overridden Meta-analysis, Health Informatics J 2024
+56.9% commission errors when clinicians are shown incorrect decision support, versus no support at all Automation-bias prescribing studies, BMC Med Inform Decis Mak 2017
70% the reliability floor below which automation makes performance worse than no automation Human-factors literature on automation reliance

Read together these say something uncomfortable about the component you have drawn. A floating assistant is, by construction, an interruption generator sitting on top of a workflow where interruptions cost measurable patient harm. Its predecessors in this exact position — pop-up CDS alerts — were ignored by clinicians more than nine times out of ten. And when a decision-support tool is wrong, clinicians do not merely fail to catch it; they make more errors than they would have made with no tool at all.

None of that is an argument against building it. It is an argument about which version to build, and it produces six laws that the rest of this document obeys.

Law 1 — Pull, not push

Consult speaks when spoken to. The dock is always visible and never proactive: no unsolicited suggestions, no badge counts, no "I noticed something about this patient". The override-rate data is the entire history of push-based clinical software, and it ends the same way every time. If a future release must push, it earns that through §17's telemetry — a proactive prompt ships only after its acceptance rate is measured above a threshold in shadow mode, never on a product manager's confidence.

Law 2 — Verification must be cheaper than acceptance

This is the load-bearing one. Automation bias is not a character flaw; it is a rational response to effort asymmetry. If accepting an answer costs one click and checking it costs four clicks and a new tab, people will accept. The design job is to invert that: the source passage is already on screen, one keystroke away, pre-scrolled to the relevant sentence. Every millisecond removed from the verification path is a direct reduction in commission errors.

Design consequence

The "Show sources" drawer must open in under 150 ms from cache, not fetch on click. Which means citations are resolved during streaming, not after. Which means the provider contract has to carry citation events inline (§15). One behavioural finding just constrained the network protocol — that is what it looks like when the behavioural work is done before the architecture rather than after.

Law 3 — Confidence must be visible and must vary

A model that answers everything in the same assured register teaches clinicians to trust everything equally, which is the definition of miscalibrated trust. Consult renders three distinct answer states — grounded (claims tied to retrieved sources), general (model knowledge, no retrieval, visibly marked), and declined (out of scope) — with different typography, not just different words. A clinician should be able to tell which of the three they are looking at from across the room.

Law 4 — The 70% floor is a release gate, not a metric

If the assembled stack — model plus retrieval plus prompt — cannot clear the reliability threshold on your own clinical eval set, shipping it makes your users worse than shipping nothing. This belongs in CI as a failing test, not on a dashboard as a number someone might look at. §17 specifies it.

Law 5 — Never interrupt a procedure

The Westbrook finding is specifically about interruption during a task. Consult must therefore know when the clinician is mid-procedure — medication administration, an active order signature, a documented timeout — and suppress itself entirely: no dock, no dictation indicator, no motion. The host application signals this through a single prop, and the honest framing for customers is that the most valuable thing this component does is disappear.

Law 6 — Design for the fiftieth use, not the first

Demos optimise for the first interaction; clinical software lives or dies on the fiftieth. Two consequences. The delightful things — the waveform, the streaming cursor, the reasoning reveal — must all be silenceable, because what reads as polished on Monday reads as latency on Friday. And the fast path must be keyboard-complete: summon, mode, shortcut, dictate, send, cite, insert, close, all without a mouse. The clinician who uses this thirty times a shift is the one whose opinion determines renewal.

What states 07 and 08 add, and why they are not optional

07 · Sources is the drawer behind "Show sources". Notice what it contains: the actual retrieved sentence with the relevant clause highlighted, the retrieval date, the guideline version, and the match score. A clinician can confirm or reject the claim without leaving the panel. That is Law 2 made concrete, and it is the difference between a component that satisfies the FDA's fourth criterion and one that gestures at it.

08 · Crisis is the deterministic interrupt. In behavioral health the model does not get to decide whether a message indicates risk — a classifier running before the model decides, and on a positive it replaces the answer entirely rather than prefixing it with a hotline number (§10). The layout matters: the model's response is not rendered at all, because a crisis message with a helpful answer attached underneath is a crisis message the user will scroll past.

Section 07

Modes are scope contracts, not prompt presets

This is the single most important architectural idea in the brief, and it is worth more than the rest of the UI combined.

The obvious implementation of your three chips is that each one swaps a system prompt. That is how almost everyone builds it, and it is wrong in a way that only shows up in the security review, eighteen months in, when someone asks which data a given mode can reach and the answer is "whatever the prompt happened to say".

A mode should instead be a declarative contract with four parts, enforced in code: what it may read, what tools it may call, what it may output, and what risk class it carries. The prompt is a fifth, subordinate field. With that shape, "can Look up see the problem list?" has an answer you can read off a config file and hand to a compliance officer, rather than one you have to infer from English.

modes.config.ts 
export const workUp: ConsultMode = {
  id: "work-up",
  label: "Work up",
  // What the model may be shown. Enforced by the context assembler,
  // not by asking the model nicely.
  reads: ["Condition", "Observation", "MedicationRequest", "AllergyIntolerance"],
  excludes: ["part2", "psychotherapy-notes", "genetic"],
  // Empty by default. A mode with no tools cannot act on the world.
  tools: [],
  // The output contract. Violations are caught post-stream and downgraded
  // to the "general" register rather than rendered as clinical claims.
  output: { requireCitations: true, maxClaims: 6, forbidDosing: true },
  // Drives the confirmation UI, the audit detail level, and whether the
  // mode is available at all under a given org policy.
  risk: "clinical",
  prompt: { ref: "work-up@3" },
};

The three modes, ranked by how much they can hurt someone

ModeRiskWhat it really isShip
Look up low Retrieval over an evidence corpus with no patient context at all. The model summarises sources it was given. Nearly all the demonstrable value, almost none of the risk. First
Prepare medium Summarisation of the record for a clinician about to walk into a room. Reads PHI, but produces no recommendation — it restates what is already documented. Failure mode is omission, which is real but bounded. Second
Work up high Differentials, investigations, referral thresholds. This is the one that generates novel clinical recommendations, and the one where a wrong answer produces the +56.9% commission-error effect from §4. Last, flagged, after evals
On differential lists specifically

Order is not neutral. A ranked differential anchors the clinician on the first item and measurably narrows what they consider afterwards. If Work up ships, present the list unranked and alphabetical, or ranked with the ranking basis stated — and consider surfacing "what would change my mind" rather than "what is most likely". The second framing is the one that survives contact with a diagnostician.

Shipping in risk order is also the commercial path of least resistance. Look up needs no BAA covering patient data in the model call, no chart integration, and no institutional review to pilot. A design partner can have it running in an afternoon. Prepare needs the chart. Work up needs a clinical governance conversation. Building them in that order means revenue arrives before the hard conversations do.

Section 08

Context, provenance, and the scope strip

The element I added to your dock is a thin grey strip nobody would put in a pitch deck. It is the highest-value pixel in the component.

A clinician looking at a floating assistant has one question they cannot currently answer: what does this thing know about my patient right now? Without an answer they must assume either too much (and trust a summary built from partial data) or too little (and stop using it). Neither is recoverable through better answers.

The scope strip answers it continuously, in three parts: who the context is about, what categories are included, and what is missing and why. That third part is the one everyone omits and the one that matters most.

The silent-omission failure

A patient has an SUD treatment episode under 42 CFR Part 2, and the copilot is not permitted to see it. If Consult summarises the record without saying so, the clinician reads a summary that appears complete and is not. The system has now actively created a false belief that would not have existed if the tool did not exist. "2 records withheld" is not a privacy nicety; it is the difference between a redaction and a lie.

Context assembly is a core concern, not an app concern

The host supplies a context resolver, not a context blob. Consult asks for exactly the categories the active mode declares, the host returns what policy allows plus an explicit list of what it withheld and why, and core assembles the prompt from that. The important property: the component cannot accidentally send data the mode did not ask for, because it never holds it.

context.ts 
interface ConsultContextResolver {
  resolve(request: {
    subject: Reference;              // Patient/123
    categories: ResourceType[];      // from mode.reads
    purpose: "treatment";            // recorded in the AuditEvent
  }): Promise<{
    resources: FhirResource[];
    // Required, not optional. An empty array is a claim of completeness
    // and must be made deliberately.
    withheld: Array<{
      reason: "part2" | "psychotherapy-notes" | "break-glass-required"
            | "minor-confidential" | "patient-restriction" | "policy";
      count: number;
      // Never the content, never enough to infer it.
      disclosable: boolean;
    }>;
    asOf: string;                     // staleness is a clinical fact
  }>;
}
Note the disclosable flag

In some jurisdictions the existence of a withheld record is itself protected — knowing a patient has a sealed record can be as revealing as reading it. So the resolver decides whether the strip says "2 records withheld" or says nothing at all, and Consult renders whichever it is told. That judgement belongs to the deploying organisation's counsel, not to a component author, and the API shape says so.

What comes back out

Every exchange produces a provenance record whether or not anyone looks at it. Oxygen already has the FHIR helpers to shape this, and Signature already established the pattern of mapping a component's value into FHIR resources rather than inventing a format:

  • AuditEvent — who asked, what mode, what was read, what was withheld, which model and version, when.
  • Provenance — attached to anything inserted into the record, with agent.who set to the clinician and the model recorded as agent.onBehalfOf. Never the reverse.
  • DocumentReference — optional, for organisations that retain transcripts. Worth telling customers plainly that a retained transcript is discoverable in litigation, because most of them have not thought about it.
Section 09

Actions: propose, confirm, attribute

The moment Consult can do something rather than say something, it becomes a different product with a different risk profile. One pattern makes that safe, and it has no exceptions.

Every action follows three steps that cannot be collapsed. The model emits a proposal — a structured object, never free text. The UI renders it as a reviewable diff with the reasoning and sources attached. A human confirms, and the confirmation is a deliberate act distinguishable from dismissing a dialog. The host then executes and writes provenance attributing the act to the human.

The temptation to collapse this is enormous and always framed as saving clicks. It is worth naming the specific failure it prevents: an agent with standing write access to an EHR, reading a patient-portal message containing a hidden instruction, executing it before any human sees the message. That is not hypothetical — it is the documented indirect-injection pattern in §12, and the propose/confirm boundary is the control that makes it survivable.

Safe to propose
  • Draft note text for review
  • Suggested problem-list additions
  • Candidate order sets, unsigned
  • Patient-education material
  • Referral letter drafts
Confirm with friction
  • Anything with a dose or a route
  • Anything that leaves the organisation
  • Anything a patient will read
  • Anything touching a Part 2 record
Never
  • Signing an order
  • Sending a message as the clinician
  • Modifying an existing note
  • Any write with no human in the loop
Design the confirmation, not just the button

A confirm step that people click through reflexively is worse than none, because it launders the model's output as human judgement while adding no scrutiny. Two things make it real: the diff must show what changes, not restate what was proposed; and the default focus must not be on the confirm control. If the telemetry in §17 shows a median confirm time under two seconds, that step is theatre and should be reported as such.

Section 10

Behavioral health

You asked specifically how this lands in behavioral health. The honest answer is that it is the domain where this component has the most to offer and the least room for error, and where a design that works fine in cardiology becomes unlawful.

Three structural differences that break the general design

The record is part of the treatment. In most of medicine, notes describe care that happened elsewhere. In psychotherapy the note frequently is the clinical material, which is why HIPAA gives psychotherapy notes their own heightened protection and why 42 CFR Part 2 now defines SUD counselling notes as a separate category requiring specific consent rather than a broad treatment authorisation. A summariser pointed at a behavioral health chart is not doing the same thing it does in a medical chart.

The patient may be the user. Everywhere else in this brief the user is a clinician. Behavioral health is the one domain where product teams routinely put the assistant in front of the patient — between sessions, at 2am, when the therapist is unreachable. That single change moves the component across a legal line that three US states have now drawn explicitly.

Risk is acute, and the window is minutes. A wrong answer about hypertension plays out over months and is recoverable. A wrong response to someone disclosing suicidal intent is not. Everything in this section follows from that asymmetry.

The legal wall, as of August 2026

Three states have legislated, they do not agree with each other, and the trajectory is toward more rather than fewer. Any behavioral health deployment of Consult has to be configurable across all three postures.

The design conclusion

Consult ships behavioral health as clinician-facing only. The patient-facing configuration is not a v2 feature to be sequenced later; it is a different product with a different regulatory footing, and putting both behind one flag invites a customer to flip it in a state where that is a five-figure penalty. If Oxygen ever builds the patient-facing version it should carry a different name and a different package.

The crisis interrupt

Mockup state 08. The design rules here are narrow and each one exists because products have failed at it in ways that made the news.

  • The classifier runs before the model, not after. If the model generates the answer and a filter inspects it, the crisis path depends on the model having behaved. It must not.
  • The answer is replaced, not annotated. Appending a hotline number to a helpful answer produces something people scroll past. Recent evaluations found safety banners disappearing entirely when unrelated clinical content was added to the same message — the failure mode is dilution, and replacement is the only reliable defence against it.
  • Guardrails must not decay across a long conversation. Measured degradation over extended sessions is well documented; medical disclaimers in one longitudinal analysis fell from 26% of responses in 2022 to under 1% by 2025. The classifier therefore evaluates every turn against the whole thread, not the latest message.
  • The hotline is resolved by locale, never hardcoded. 988 works in the United States and nowhere else. A component that ships 988 as a constant is shipping a bug to every other market, and it is the kind of bug that gets discovered by the person least able to absorb it.
  • Escalation is to a human, and the component says so plainly. The interstitial copy should not be warm. Warmth here reads as the machine continuing to play a role it must exit.
Test it against something real

VERA-MH — Validation of Ethical and Responsible AI in Mental Health, released by Spring Health with an expert council — is an open-source, clinically grounded evaluation for exactly this. Its human-validation study reported an LLM judge aligned with clinical consensus at 0.81 chance-corrected agreement, against 0.77 between the clinicians themselves. That makes it usable as an automated CI gate rather than a report someone reads once. Wire it into §17's harness and the crisis path has evidence behind it instead of a design intention.

Where the real value is: measurement-based care

The high-value behavioral health use for this component is not therapy and not diagnosis. It is the thing every behavioral health organisation says it wants and under a fifth actually do consistently — measurement-based care. PHQ-9, GAD-7, AUDIT-C, PCL-5, C-SSRS, WHODAS, collected on schedule and actually looked at before the session.

A Prepare-mode shortcut that opens with "PHQ-9 down 14→8 over three visits, GAD-7 flat at 12, last completed 6 days ago, two items unanswered" is genuinely useful, is purely a restatement of documented data, generates no clinical recommendation, sits outside every one of the three state laws above, and takes a week to build. It is also the kind of thing a psychiatrist notices on day one and misses when it is gone.

The behavioral health mode pack that follows from this is deliberately unambitious:

Mode · low risk

Between visits

Instrument trends, missed appointments, adherence signals, outstanding measures. Reads only what was documented; recommends nothing.

Mode · low risk

Look up

Formulary, interactions, guideline text, titration schedules. No patient context. The safest surface in the product, and the one clinicians ask for most.

Mode · high risk

Formulate

Case formulation support. Genuinely valuable, genuinely dangerous, and the closest thing to what Illinois prohibits. Behind a flag, clinician-facing, evals first.

Two smaller things that signal you have done this before

Language. Behavioral health has a specific vocabulary problem: "addict", "clean", "committed suicide", "non-compliant", "manipulative" — each carries clinical judgement that changes how the next clinician reads the patient. Oxygen already ships an eslint-plugin package, which makes a no-stigmatising-language rule over the message catalog a genuinely cheap and unusually credible addition. The same list belongs in the output post-check, flagging the model's language, not just the component's own strings.

Silence during the session. Therapeutic alliance is the strongest single predictor of outcome in psychotherapy, and it is built through attention. A dock that pulses, streams, or animates during a session is competing with the thing that actually works. Law 5's suppressed prop is not an edge case here — for a therapy product it is the default state, and the component should be visible in the ten minutes before and the ten minutes after, not the fifty in between.

Section 11

The regulatory surface

Four regimes touch this component. None of them regulate Oxygen — they regulate the customer. That distinction is the entire commercial argument: the component's job is to make the customer's compliance work cheap, and to never be the reason they fail it.

FDA — Clinical Decision Support software

The FDA revised its CDS guidance twice in January 2026, most recently on 29 January. Software escapes device regulation only if it meets all four non-device criteria, and the fourth is the one this component lives or dies on: the software must enable the healthcare professional to independently review the basis for the recommendations, so that they do not rely primarily on it to make a diagnosis or treatment decision.

Four things in the 2026 revision matter for the design:

  1. Single recommendations are now workable. The 2022 guidance effectively pushed developers to output lists; FDA now intends enforcement discretion where only one option is clinically appropriate. Consult does not need to manufacture fake alternatives to stay non-device.
  2. Time-critical use moved into criterion 4. The reasoning is explicit and it is a design instruction: a clinician under time pressure cannot independently review anything. Consult must therefore know when it is in a time-critical context and withdraw — which is the same suppressed prop as Law 5, now doing regulatory work as well as human-factors work.
  3. Sources must be well understood and accepted. Established guidelines and peer-reviewed literature — not novel data sources, not proprietary derived signals. This constrains what a customer may put in the retrieval corpus, and Consult should make corpus provenance visible enough that they notice when they have crossed it.
  4. Avoid information overload. The guidance now explicitly asks that decision-relevant details be prioritised. An answer that buries the clinically relevant sentence under five paragraphs is not merely bad UX; it works against the criterion the whole design depends on.
The gap worth telling customers about

Despite being framed as an AI-focused update, the 2026 guidance is silent on generative AI, and it declines to address consumer-facing decision support entirely. So there is no safe harbour for an LLM-based clinical assistant and no policy at all for a patient-facing one. Customers who assume the guidance blesses their chatbot have misread it, and saying so early is worth more to them than agreeing.

ONC / ASTP — HTI-1 Decision Support Interventions

If a customer's product is certified health IT, the DSI criterion at § 170.315(b)(11) applies. It requires 31 source attributes for predictive DSIs — the "model card" set: development details, validation, fairness, performance, maintenance — plus intervention risk management practices covering risk analysis, mitigation and governance along the lines of the NIST AI Risk Management Framework. Certified developers had to meet this by 31 December 2024 and maintain it from 1 January 2025.

This is a product opportunity, not a burden

Consult's disclosure sheet should be structured to the 31 attributes, shipped as a typed object the customer fills in, rendered by the component, and exportable. A customer pursuing certification then gets most of a compliance artifact from a component they installed for the UI. That is the kind of thing that turns a component purchase into a platform decision — and no free chat widget will do it.

EU AI Act

Article 50's transparency obligations came into force on 2 August 2026 — two weeks ago — and were explicitly not deferred by the Digital Omnibus package that pushed stand-alone high-risk Annex III obligations back to 2 December 2027. So the chatbot-disclosure duty is live now while the heavier high-risk regime is not.

Practically: a user must know they are interacting with an AI system, and AI-generated content carries marking obligations (with a grace period to 2 December 2026 for systems already on the market before August). Consult's standing disclosure line — the one already in your screenshot 6 — is the right primitive. It needs to be non-dismissible, locale-aware, and present on the patient-facing surface if one ever exists.

HIPAA, 42 CFR Part 2, and the BAA question

  • The model provider needs a BAA if PHI reaches it. This is the first question a customer's security team asks and Consult should make the answer structural: the provider adapter carries a phiPermitted flag, and core refuses to attach patient context to a provider not marked as covered. A configuration error becomes a thrown exception rather than a breach.
  • Part 2 enforcement began 16 February 2026. OCR announced its civil enforcement program on 13 February and began accepting complaints three days later. The rule states that segregating Part 2 records is not required — which means customers may hold them commingled, which means Consult cannot assume the host's data is already filtered. The excludes list in §7 and the withheld array in §8 exist for exactly this.
  • Never put the API key in the browser. Obvious, and still worth an explicit line in the README, because the single most common integration mistake with any model SDK is a client-side key. The adapter targets the customer's own endpoint; Consult has no network configuration that could reach a model vendor directly.
Section 12

Security: the chart is hostile input

This is the failure mode most likely to produce a headline, and the one most teams building this component have not thought about at all.

Indirect prompt injection is the attack where instructions are hidden inside content the model retrieves rather than in what the user types. In healthcare the retrieval corpus is the chart, the patient-portal message queue, uploaded PDFs, and referral letters — all of which contain text written by people outside the organisation. One 2025 simulation study reported commercial medical LLMs following injected instructions in 94.4% of simulated patient encounters, including recommendations that would have been life-threatening.

The reason generic defences fail here is worth stating precisely: the dangerous requests in a clinical setting are fluent and legitimate-looking. "Export this patient's chart to the covering physician" carries no jailbreak signal. An injection classifier scores it benign because, as a sentence, it is benign. What makes it an attack is that nobody authorised it.

The containment rules
  • Two channels, never merged. Instructions come from the mode's prompt template and the user's typed question. Chart content enters as fenced, labelled data with an explicit "this is a record, not an instruction" frame. They are concatenated in core, in one place, that has a test.
  • Retrieved content is never rendered as markup. A note containing HTML or a link renders as text. Injection via the display layer is the same bug in a different coat.
  • Tools are allowlisted per mode and empty by default. §7. An agent that cannot call anything cannot be made to exfiltrate anything.
  • Egress is host-mediated. Consult makes no outbound request except to the adapter endpoint the host configured. There is no second network path to subvert.
What Consult does not solve

A component cannot secure a backend. Consult can guarantee the shape of what it sends and the provenance of what it renders; it cannot stop a customer wiring an agent with standing EHR write access behind it. The documentation should say this in the same plain register as Oxygen's existing "not a compliance boundary" line — and §9's propose/confirm pattern is the mitigation Oxygen can ship.

Worth tracking rather than adopting: healthcare-specific work on positive-security prompt firewalls and the MPIB medical injection benchmark, both 2026. Neither is mature enough to depend on, and both are the right shape for a later consult-guard package.

Section 13

Accessibility

Oxygen's differentiator is that its components are accessible where Ant Design's are not. A streaming chat surface is where that claim gets tested hardest, because the naive implementation is genuinely unusable with a screen reader.

The streaming problem, and the actual answer

Putting aria-live="polite" on a streaming response container produces a screen reader that either reads every partial token — an unusable stutter — or silently coalesces and reads nothing useful. Both are common in shipped products. The working pattern has four parts:

  1. The streaming region is aria-live="off" and aria-busy="true" while text arrives. Nothing is announced during the stream.
  2. A separate visually-hidden status region announces state transitions only: "Answering…", then on completion "Answer complete. 240 words, 3 sources."
  3. On completion, aria-busy flips to false and focus management offers the answer — the user moves to it deliberately rather than being dragged.
  4. A preference for progressive announcement exists for users who want it, announcing at sentence boundaries rather than tokens. Off by default, because the majority preference is the summary.

The rest of the surface

ConcernRequirement
Dock semanticsThe dock is a complementary landmark with an accessible name, not a bare floating div. It must be reachable in the tab order at a predictable point and escapable from.
Panel focusOn open, focus moves to the panel heading; on close it returns to the element that opened it. The panel is not a dialog and must not trap focus — a clinician has to be able to keep working in the chart with it open.

Section 14

Architecture

Consult also happens to settle the open question Signature left behind — whether Oxygen wraps Ant Design, matches it, or migrates. The answer this component forces is better than any of the three.

Resolving the registry-versus-antd tension

Signature took a dependency on antd because antd's Modal focus trap and Tabs ARIA were worth more than reimplementing them. That was the right call there and it is the wrong call here, for a simple reason: almost none of Consult is an antd component. A floating dock, a streaming thread, a citation drawer, a waveform, a slash palette — antd has none of these. The antd surface Consult would actually consume is Drawer, Dropdown, Tooltip and Button, which is a rounding error against the bespoke work.

So Consult should ship headless, with skins on top. This is not a compromise between wrap and match; it is the structure that makes the question stop mattering.

Four packages
  • consult-core — engine. No React, no DOM, no dependencies. Exactly the signature-core precedent, and for the same reason: the expensive half of the work stays portable.
  • consult-react — headless React. Hooks and unstyled primitives that own state, keyboard, focus and ARIA. Peer dependency on React only.
  • consult — the antd skin. Peer dependency on antd, thin.
  • registry consult — the Tailwind/registry skin, copy-as-source, depending on consult-react from npm.
Why this is worth the extra package

The accessibility work in §13 — the streaming announcement strategy, focus handling, the combobox — is the hardest part of the component and is design-system-agnostic. Putting it in consult-react means it is written once and correct in both channels, instead of written twice and correct in neither.

It also means the copy-as-source registry channel survives, which the wrap option would have ended. That matters for adoption: developers try the registry, and buy the packages.

The engine

consult-core is a reducer plus a middleware pipeline. It has no timers it does not own, no globals, and no I/O it did not receive as an argument, which is what makes it testable without a browser.

consult-core/src/session.ts — the state chart

Two properties of this chart are load-bearing. crisis is terminal — there is no edge back to streaming, so no code path exists in which a crisis classification is followed by a model answer. And committed is reachable only through confirming, so §9's rule is enforced by the type system rather than by developer discipline.

The pipeline

Every exchange runs the same ordered chain. Each stage is pluggable, each stage's decision is recorded, and the order is not configurable — that is the point.

Where the model lives

The rule that makes "configurable with any model" true

Consult never talks to a model vendor. The adapter targets an endpoint the customer operates, and that endpoint holds the key, applies the BAA-covered routing, and enforces rate limits and tenancy. This is what makes the component genuinely model-agnostic — swapping providers is a change on the customer's server, not a change to the component — and it removes the single most common integration vulnerability at the same time. Oxygen ships a reference endpoint as an example, never as a service.

Section 16

Implementation plan

Sequenced by risk, not by visibility. Every phase ends with something a design partner can run, which is the only sequencing that survives contact with a real customer. Estimates assume one strong engineer; the clinical review in phases 4 and 6 is not engineering time and cannot be compressed by adding engineers.

  1. Phase 0 · Decide and spike ~1 week

    Nothing in this brief is worth building until §18's four decisions are answered. The spike exists to make one of them cheap to answer.

    • Build the dock and a streaming thread twice — once headless, once on antd — and time both. This settles the §14 recommendation with evidence rather than argument.
    • Write consult.meta.ts against the existing schema and run pnpm gen. If the metadata does not express a mode registry, the schema needs a field before code is written, not after.
    • Draft the ModelDisclosure type against the 31 HTI-1 attributes. This is a research task with a typed artifact at the end of it, and it is the piece most likely to be underestimated.

  2. Phase 1 · Core engine and the dock ~3 weeks

    Look up mode only. No patient context anywhere in the codebase. The lowest-risk slice, and the one a design partner can pilot without an institutional review.

    • consult-core: session reducer, provider contract, event normaliser, mode registry, pipeline skeleton with scope and stream stages live and the rest as pass-throughs.
    • consult-react: useConsult, dock primitives, slash combobox, dictation via browser SpeechRecognition behind the same adapter shape the real ASR will use.
    • Reference endpoint as an example app under apps/, wired to an OpenAI-compatible shape so it works against most vendors unchanged.
    • Tests: reducer transitions exhaustively, event normaliser against three malformed stream fixtures, axe on every dock state.

  3. Phase 2 · Panel, citations, disclosure ~3 weeks

    • Panel, thread, streaming render with the §13 announcement strategy — budget real time for this; it is the hardest accessibility work in the component.
    • Inline citation markers, sources drawer, claim-to-marker mapping, the "general" register for uncited spans.
    • ConsultDisclosure rendering the HTI-1 attribute set, exportable.
    • The five required non-happy states from Oxygen's DESIGN.md, designed rather than defaulted.
    • Playwright VRT at 320px and 400% zoom, added to the existing @vrt tag.

  4. Phase 3 · Patient context ~3 weeks

    Unlocks Prepare mode. This is where the component starts touching PHI and where the security conversation with customers begins.

    • Context resolver contract, assembler, the withheld path end to end including the disclosable distinction.
    • Scope strip. Injection containment in assemble, with the instruction/data fence covered by a test that fails loudly if the two channels are ever concatenated elsewhere.
    • AuditEvent and Provenance mapping via @oxygenui-design/fhir.
    • The phiPermitted guard, with a test asserting it throws rather than degrades.

  5. Phase 4 · Behavioral health pack ~2 weeks + clinical review

    • Crisis classifier interface, interstitial, locale-resolved crisis lines, terminal state enforcement.
    • Part 2 exclusion category wired through modes and the resolver.
    • "Between visits" mode with instrument trend rendering — PHQ-9, GAD-7, C-SSRS.
    • no-stigmatising-language ESLint rule in the existing eslint-plugin, plus the same list as an output post-check.
    • VERA-MH wired into CI. Clinical review gate before merge, not after.

  6. Phase 5 · Actions ~3 weeks

    • Proposal type, diff rendering, confirm step with anti-reflex defaults, attributed commit, undo window.
    • Per-mode tool allowlist enforced in core.
    • Confirm-dwell telemetry from day one, so §9's theatre check has data.

  7. Phase 6 · Work up only after §17 passes

    • Behind a flag, off by default, clinician-facing only, unranked differentials.
    • Ships when the eval gate clears, not when the sprint ends. If that is never, that is an acceptable outcome and the product is still good.

Roughly fourteen to sixteen weeks to a defensible v1 covering phases 0–4, with 5 and 6 following on evidence. Phases 2 and 3 partially parallelise across two engineers; phase 4 does not, because it is gated on a clinician's calendar rather than on code.

The estimate most likely to be wrong

The HTI-1 disclosure work in phase 0/2 looks like typing a type. It is actually gathering 31 pieces of information about a model, some of which the customer's vendor will not readily supply. Budget it as research, expect it to surface uncomfortable questions, and treat those questions as the product working.

Section 17

Evals and telemetry

The part that separates a component someone bought from a component someone trusts. Both halves belong in the repository, not in a dashboard.

The release gate

Law 4, as a failing test

Below roughly 70% reliability, automation makes human performance worse than no automation at all. So the assembled stack must clear that on the customer's own eval set before a mode is enabled — and Consult should ship the harness that measures it, with the gate wired into CI as a test that fails the build. A number on a dashboard is a number nobody blocks a release on.

Telemetry, and the metric that must not be the north star

Engagement is the wrong goal here

For a consumer chat product, more messages is better. For this component it is ambiguous at best: a clinician sending forty messages a shift may be getting enormous value, or may have been pulled into a tool that is now the work rather than helping with it. Optimise for task completion time and verification rate. Track engagement as a diagnostic, never as a success metric — and say so out loud when a customer's growth team asks for the opposite.

Signals worth acting on
  • Verification rate — proportion of answers where the sources drawer was opened. Falling over time is the automation-bias early warning, and it is the single most important number in the product.
  • Time to verify — Law 2 made measurable. If it climbs, the drawer has regressed.
  • Refusal rate, split by correct and incorrect — over-refusal drives abandonment silently.
  • Confirm dwell time — median under two seconds means §9's confirm step has become theatre.
  • Abandonment after first token — the answer started badly.
Shipped as hooks, not a service

Consult emits typed telemetry events and stores nothing. The customer routes them wherever their analytics already live. Oxygen never becomes a processor of clinical interaction data — that is a liability position, a procurement obstacle, and a data-residency conversation nobody in a component library wants to have.

The same reasoning applies to transcripts, threads and audit events. Every one of them goes out through a host sink.

Section 18

Risks, open decisions, and what I would cut

Four decisions that need your answer before code

  1. The four-package headless split (§14) — yes or no? This is the Signature question coming back. My recommendation is yes, and Consult is the right component to settle it on because so little of it is antd. Saying no means the registry channel ends for anything sophisticated, which is a strategy decision, not a packaging one.
  2. Where is the free/pro line? My suggestion: the dock, the thread, and Look up mode are free and in the registry — that is the adoption engine. Context resolution, audit mapping, the disclosure sheet, the eval harness, and the behavioral health pack are pro. The free tier must be genuinely useful or it does no work; the pro tier must be the compliance machinery, because that is what has a budget attached.
  3. Does Oxygen ship a reference backend? An example app is safe and clearly scoped. A supported package edges Oxygen toward being infrastructure, which is a different company. I would ship the example and resist the package for at least a year, but it is a strategy call, not a technical one.
  4. Is behavioral health a mode pack or a separate product? §10 argues that the patient-facing configuration must be a different package with a different name. The clinician-facing behavioral health pack can live inside Consult. Confirm you agree, because the answer changes the package layout in phase 0.

Risks worth naming now

Scope drift into the scribe category

Dictation is one small step from ambient recording, and a customer will ask. That market has Abridge, Microsoft DAX and Nabla in it with hundreds of millions in funding and deep Epic integration — together they hold the large majority of ambient deployments in Epic hospitals. Oxygen cannot win it and should not try. Say no early and in writing.

Liability positioning

Shipping safety machinery invites the reading that Oxygen is warranting safety. The existing "not a compliance boundary" language needs a Consult-specific extension, reviewed by someone qualified, before the first sale rather than after the first incident.

The regulatory content ages

§11 moved twice in January 2026 alone, and Part 2 enforcement began in February. Documentation that states the law will be wrong within a year. Write it as "what this component does" with the regulation as rationale, so a change in the rule does not invalidate the docs.

Name collision, again

"Consult" is common in health IT. It is defensible as a component name inside a namespaced library and it is not a product brand, so this is a lower risk than the Oxygen name itself already carries — but check npm and the trademark register in phase 0 rather than at launch.

What I would cut from your sketch

  • Multi-select modes. §3. An unreviewed permission union.
  • "Ask anything…". §3. Promises a scope the component must refuse.
  • Work up, from v1. §7. Highest harm, lowest confidence, and the mode most likely to be demoed before it is evaluated.
  • The reasoning panel as a default-open surface. Keep it collapsed. Visible chain-of-thought reads as evidence to a clinician, and it is not evidence — it is a narrative the model produced alongside the answer.

What I would add that you did not ask for

  • The scope strip. §8. The highest-value element in the component.
  • The suppressed prop. §4 Law 5 and §11's time-critical clause, one prop serving both.
  • Sources as passages, not links. §5 state 07. The difference between satisfying criterion 4 and gesturing at it.
  • The eval harness as a shipped package. §17. Nobody else selling a component does this, and it is what makes the whole argument credible.
The bottom line

Your instinct is right — this is the surface every clinical application is about to need, and being early with a good one is worth a great deal. But the version worth building is not the version that demos best. It is the one that makes a clinician faster at checking, that can prove where an answer came from, that knows when to disappear, and that treats behavioral health as its own domain rather than a theme. That component is roughly four months of work and it has no real competitor. The one that demos best is three weeks and has a hundred.
