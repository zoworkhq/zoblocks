Section 01

The waveform is instrumentation, not decoration

Every voice-recorder interface on the web animates on a timer. In a consulting room that is not a stylistic choice — it is a failure mode with a name, a cost, and a victim.

Here is the whole problem in one sentence. A clinician starts an ambient recording, talks to a patient for twenty-two minutes, and the bars dance the entire time. At sign-off there is no transcript, because the operating system had the microphone muted from the second minute. The interface was animating a setInterval, not a signal, and it had no way to know the difference.

This is not hypothetical and it is not rare. The three commonest causes — an OS-level input mute, a Bluetooth headset that switched profile, and a device that disappeared and was silently replaced by the built-in microphone — all produce the same thing at the interface layer: frames of digital silence, arriving on schedule. A timer-driven waveform is by construction incapable of distinguishing that from a room where nobody is currently speaking, and those two facts have opposite consequences.

That claim is easy to make and easy to wave through, so here it is as running code instead. Both lanes below are real. The left one is forty-eight bars on staggered CSS animations — the standard implementation, and one you will recognise. The right one is the same markup with its bar heights written from the analyser.

The claim

The art must be a pure function of the signal. No timer, no phase, no clock of its own. If the analyser reports nothing, the component must render nothing moving — and then it must say so, because a perfectly flat waveform and a quiet room look identical, and only one of them is a fault.

That single constraint is the spine of everything below. It decides the engine (an AnalyserNode, not a requestAnimationFrame counter), the failure catalogue (§11), the accessibility answer (§10), and even the reduced-motion still state (§09) — because when the motion is carrying the only evidence that the microphone is open, you cannot simply stop it and call the job done.

It also decides how this document is built. Every waveform on this page runs through one signal source and one animation loop, exactly as the component is specified. The buttons in §04 change that source. Nothing else on the page has a clock, so nothing else can keep dancing when you take the signal away.

Section 02

Four jobs, one component

Recording, listening, transcribing and sending look like four components. They are four readings of the same array, and treating them as one is what makes ten variants affordable.

The brief that started this asked for “recording or listening” — used for recording and send, and for listening and transcribe. That is four jobs, and the instinct is four components. It is the wrong cut, and the reason is worth stating precisely because it is what makes the whole family cheap.

01
Record
A Float32Array of peak buckets is being appended to. The newest bucket is the right edge. Duration is unknown and growing.
02
Listen
The same Float32Array, complete, is being indexed into. The playhead is a cursor over it. Duration is known.
03
Transcribe
A second stream, time-aligned to the first, arriving as revisable tokens. It is the array's caption track, not a separate surface.
04
Send
Not a view at all — a disposition. Where the bytes currently are, and what still has to happen to them. §11 and the Stack composition.

Record and listen differ by which end of the same buffer you read. That is the entire difference. A player is a recorder whose array stopped growing. Once you see it that way, a waveform renderer written for one works unmodified for the other, the peaks format is shared, the speaker lanes are shared, and the marker model is shared — which is why ten designs cost roughly what three would have.

Transcribe is not a fifth job, it is a second track on the same clock. And send is not a view; it is the answer to “where is this recording right now”, which no waveform can express and which almost every recorder omits.

Precedent

This is the same call the date brief made — one temporal control with fourteen variants rather than fourteen controls — and the same call the loaders made: five arts over one OxLoaderElement, sharing a timing gate, an attribute contract and one stylesheet. The five recorder arts in §05 are deliberately the same number, over the same kind of base.

Section 03

Three rules, and they are the component

Everything else here is implementation. These three are the reason to build it rather than wrap MediaRecorder in a div.

Rule one — the art is driven by the signal, or it does not move

Stated in §01, and it has a testable consequence: silence must be drawn at true zero. Not at a decorative floor of 0.05 “so it looks alive”. The centre hairline in the Bars art is real, and a bar that touches it means the recogniser is receiving nothing.

The corollary is the feature nobody ships: a silence budget. The component knows how long it has been continuously below the voice floor and says so, escalating from quiet to no voice for 20 s — check the microphone. The single most expensive failure in ambient documentation is a long recording of a muted microphone, and it is entirely preventable by a component that is already computing the number it needs.

Rule two — recording is a disclosure, so consent is a state of the component

Not a dialog in front of it. The machine in §06 has an armed phase that is not idle: permission granted, device chosen, nothing captured, and no affirmative basis recorded. recording is unreachable from armed until consent resolves, and that edge is enforced in recorder-core rather than left to whoever wires the button.

Once capturing, the fact of capturing must be continuously and unmistakably visible. This is the one place in Oxygen where motion is allowed to insist — the same exemption the interaction layer already grants .cs-crit.is-urgent, and the reason the record dot breathes when nothing else on the surface does.

Rule three — a recording is evidence before it is data

So the component must be able to say captured but not sent. This is the Switch's principle — a control that admits when the write did not land — applied to a file that may be the only record of a consultation.

The phases after stop are real and distinct and each is rendered: held, uploading, queued, transcribing, ready, failed. The important one is held. A recorder that shows a tick while the bytes are still in IndexedDB has told a clinician something false about a legal record, and it is the default behaviour of every thin MediaRecorder wrapper on npm.

What follows from rule three

A recorder holding unsent audio must not unmount quietly. It registers a beforeunload guard, keeps a visible held badge, and writes to IndexedDB as it captures rather than at the end — so a tab that dies at minute nineteen loses seconds, not everything. That is a component-level obligation, not an application-level nicety.

Section 04

The ten designs

Live, and all driven by one signal through one loop. Change the source and every figure below changes with it — which is the argument in §01, running rather than asserted.

Signal A decorative waveform looks identical in all four. Try muted — nothing below can keep dancing, because nothing below owns a clock.

There is no microphone involved. The amplitude is synthesised with a speech-shaped envelope — 4.6 Hz syllable rate, word and phrase envelopes, breath noise — because asking a reader for microphone permission to look at a design brief is not a trade anyone should make. Everything downstream of that envelope is the real specification: the attack/release asymmetry in §08, the peak buckets in §07, the voice floor, the silence budget, and the onset detector that decides when Pulse emits a ring.

Three of the ten do something a waveform is not usually asked to do, and they are the three worth arguing about. Pulse emits one sonar ring per detected syllable rather than on a loop. Orb wraps the last four seconds around a dial, so time is the angle in both the waveform and the elapsed arc. Duet puts the speaker on the axis — clinician above the line, patient below — which makes talk-time balance readable without reading anything.

Section 05

Which five ship, and why the other five are not rejections

Five arts, two compositions, three held. The cut is principled rather than scored: an art is a renderer, and two of the ten are not renderers at all.

The temptation is to rank ten designs and cut at five. That produces a defensible-looking table and a bad catalogue, because it treats a layout and a renderer as the same kind of thing. The honest cut is by kind:

5
Arts — Pulse, Bars, Strip, Duet and Stream
A renderer: takes the signal and the buffer, produces pixels. Selected with variant. These are the five, and they are deliberately the same count as the five loaders, over the same kind of shared base.
2
Compositions — Stack and Console
Not renderers. Console is Bars with the host regions filled in; Stack has no waveform and no transport at all. Counting either as a sixth art would be counting a layout twice.
3
Held — Rhythm, Orb and Caption
Each has a stated condition that would move it. None is held because it is weak; all three are held because the thing that would justify them does not exist yet.

The three that are held, and what would move them

Rhythm is the direct sibling of rhythm-loader and visually the most distinctive thing here. It is held for a reason that is not aesthetic: it is the variant most likely to be misread as clinical data. On a screen that also shows a real rhythm strip, two traces — one of them decorative — is a mistake we should not sell, and the docs cannot reliably prevent someone putting it there. It ships when an inpatient surface asks for it by name.

Orb is the one I most wanted to ship and it is held on a missing use case rather than a missing capability. The polar waveform is the best-looking thing in this document, and that is not an argument — the arc around it only means something against a declared total, and an encounter has no ten-minute ceiling. Drawing one implies a deadline nobody set. Strip the arc and it is a prettier Pulse that costs more pixels. The day there is a capped-duration surface — a timed assessment prompt, a voice message with a limit — it is two hours of work.

Caption is held on a contract we do not control. Word-level timings are not universal; several recognisers return segment timings only, and with those this degrades to phrase highlighting, which is a different and weaker control. It ships the day the recogniser contract guarantees word timings, and until then Duet plus Stream covers the job between them.

The one I would argue about

Pulse over Console is the only close call among the five. Console is the better demo and the better screenshot; Pulse is the only art that keeps a real level meter at 44 px, which is what every mobile and patient-facing surface needs. If the first paying customer is an ambient-scribe vendor on desktop, that call inverts — and it inverts cleanly, because Console would then become an art and Pulse a held design.

The one I would defend hardest is Duet, and it is also the most expensive: it wants a peaks sidecar and a speaker byte per bucket, neither of which exists today. It is worth the platform ticket. Every competitor's playback view puts speakers in a lane under the waveform; folding them onto the axis is a genuinely different reading of the same data and it is the one thing in this set nobody else is doing.

Section 06

The state machine

Eleven phases and four terminal states. The machine lives in recorder-core with no React and no DOM, so the same edges hold in a test, in a web component and on a server.

Four edges are load-bearing and none of them is obvious.

  • armed is not idle. Permission is granted and the device is chosen, but nothing is being captured and no basis is recorded. Most implementations collapse these two and then have nowhere to put the consent step. Rule two lives on this edge.
  • held is not stopping and not uploading. It is a resting state that can last for days, on a laptop in a bag. It has to survive a reload.
  • paused is not a dropped stream. Clinicians step out of the room. Pause has to be visually distinct from both stopping and a device fault, or the component teaches people to distrust all three.
  • failed is not terminal for the audio. A failed transcription is a failed transcription; the recording is intact and the step is retryable. Collapsing them loses recordings, and it is the commonest way a pipeline throws away the expensive artefact because the cheap one broke.
Section 07

The signal path, and the data model behind it

Four stages between the microphone and a pixel. Three of them are where audio components usually go wrong, and all three are arithmetic rather than taste.

01
Capture — constraints matter more than the code
getUserMedia with echoCancellation, noiseSuppression and autoGainControl made explicit. AGC must be off for the meter to mean anything — automatic gain normalises the input, so a meter reading a normalised stream reports the browser's opinion of the room rather than the room. If a product needs AGC on for quality, the meter has to say it is reading a processed signal.
02
Analyse — peak, not RMS
An AnalyserNode with getFloatTimeDomainData, reduced to a peak magnitude per frame. RMS is the right measure for perceived loudness and the wrong one for “is anything arriving”: it smooths away exactly the transients that prove the microphone is open.
03
Envelope — asymmetric, and this is §08
Instant attack, ~180 ms release. The single most common defect in audio UI is symmetric smoothing, and it is why most level meters look like lava lamps.
04
Bucket — 30 Hz peaks into a ring
A Float32Array of per-bucket peaks, written at 30 Hz. Independent of frame rate, so the waveform is identical on a 60 Hz laptop and a 120 Hz tablet — which also makes it screenshot-stable, and therefore testable.

Why buckets and not samples

A twenty-minute encounter is about 58 million samples at 48 kHz. It is 36,000 buckets at 30 Hz — 144 kB as a Float32Array, which you can hold in memory, post to a worker, serialise as a peaks sidecar, and downsample by binary reduction for an overview without ever touching the audio again.

That last point is what makes the Duet art possible at all. Computing peaks in the browser means downloading the whole take first, so a twelve-minute consultation shows a spinner before it shows a waveform. The peaks sidecar should be emitted at ingest, server side, from the same reduction — and that is a requirement this component places on the platform, not a detail it can absorb.

The sidecar carries a second lane: one byte of speaker per bucket. That is 36 kB on a twenty-minute encounter and it is what turns a waveform into Duet. Asking the platform for it at the same time as the peaks costs one field; asking for it a quarter later costs a migration.

Rendering

Below roughly 200 bars, DOM elements with one custom property each — cheap, themeable, inspectable, and what every art in §04 uses. Above that, one canvas. The threshold is not a preference: 220 <i> elements each taking a style write per frame is already measurable on a mid-range Android tablet, which is the device a community nurse actually carries.

Section 08

Motion: two numbers that decide whether it feels like an instrument

Oxygen's motion layer has four durations and two curves and no springs. A level meter needs one thing that layer does not have — an asymmetric envelope — and it needs it to be exact.

A meter that rises and falls at the same rate reads as jelly. A meter that rises instantly and falls slowly reads as an instrument, because that is what every peak-programme meter, VU meter and DAW fader has done for sixty years. The asymmetry is the whole effect.

The one place this fights the design system

Oxygen spends red on alarms. This component spends it on capturing, which is not an alarm — because fifty years of cameras, tape decks and voice-memo apps have made a red dot mean recording, and fighting that costs more than the palette collision does. The resolution is a dedicated --ox-recorder-rec that is deliberately warmer and lighter than --ox-crit, checked against it and not only against the background, so an alert raised during a recording is still the more urgent thing on screen.

Section 09

Reduced motion, when the motion is the evidence

Every other Oxygen component can simply stop. This one cannot: the movement is carrying the only proof that the microphone is open. So the answer is a substitution, not a removal.

The loaders established the rule — the still state is designed, not paused: shapes complete, the track comes to full strength, the mark breathes in opacity. That works because a loader's animation carries no information a caption could not carry instead.

A recorder's animation does. Remove it and the user loses the answer to “is it hearing me”, which is the question the component exists to answer. So under prefers-reduced-motion the design does three things, in this order:

01
The scroll stops. The meter does not.
Translation is what triggers vestibular symptoms; a single bar changing height in place does not. The lane collapses to one non-translating level bar.
02
The information the scroll carried comes back as a number.
A live dBFS readout and a tabular elapsed timer. Motion is replaced, not removed — which is the part most implementations skip.
03
The record tell stays, without the loop.
The dot keeps its ring at full strength instead of breathing. Rule two does not have a reduced-motion exemption.
A finding, not a footnote

A live scrolling waveform is very likely a WCAG 2.2.2 failure. The success criterion covers scrolling information that starts automatically, lasts more than five seconds and is presented in parallel with other content, and requires a mechanism to pause, stop or hide it — unless the movement is essential, which WCAG defines as information that cannot be conveyed in another conforming way. §09 exists precisely to prove it can. Our own reduced-motion state is the evidence that the exception does not apply.

So the component owes an explicit in-component mechanism, not only a media query: a motion prop and a visible control that reaches it. Honouring prefers-reduced-motion alone is a defensible position that I would not want to defend in an audit.

Section 10

Accessibility, for a control whose output is a picture of sound

A screen-reader user cannot see a waveform, and a live region that announces a level meter is a denial of service. Both problems have the same answer.

The naive implementation puts aria-live="polite" near the timer and produces a screen reader that says a number every second for twenty minutes. The correct implementation announces on transitions and on a slow heartbeat, and lets the user ask for the rest.

The interesting one is the pair that cannot pass, and it took the measurement to see it.

The constraint that has no colour solution

The first draft of this section claimed the played and unplayed halves of a waveform owe 3:1 against each other, because the boundary between them is the information. That claim is wrong, and the arithmetic says so.

Both fills sit on the same pane and both are darker than it, so they are on the same side of its luminance. On the light theme the pane is at L 0.89 and the played fill at L 0.15 — 4.64:1. For the unplayed fill to reach 3:1 against the played one it must land at L 0.56, which is 1.55:1 against the pane and effectively invisible. Push it dark enough to be 3:1 against the pane and it lands at 1.55:1 against the played fill. The two requirements have no common solution on a single ramp, and every audio interface that tries to satisfy both ends up failing one silently.

So the design stops trying. The unplayed fill is solved to 3:1 against the pane — that is the requirement that actually matters, because the unplayed waveform is the shape a reader aims at before clicking. The played/unplayed boundary is then carried by two things that are not a fill colour: a tinted region wash whose edge is a hard vertical line, and the playhead, which is a full-height 2 px marker with a head. The colour difference — 1.50:1 light, 2.49:1 dark — is redundancy on top of a boundary you can already see from across the room.

The same reasoning, applied to the two speakers

Accent and info sit at 2.02:1 light and 2.91:1 dark of each other — a hue distinction, which DESIGN.md forbids as a sole channel and which deuteranopia flattens. In Duet that is fine, because the speaker is carried by position first: above the axis and below it. The lightness step and the labelled key dots are the second and third channels. Had the two speakers been drawn as adjacent lanes rather than as a fold, the same colours would have been a genuine failure — which is an argument for the fold that I did not expect to find in a contrast check.

Section 12

Consent, disclosure and striking the record

Three things a recorder in a clinical setting owes that a recorder on a marketing site does not. The third one is the one nobody ships.

Consent is a state, not a modal

The armed phase in §06 exists so there is somewhere for consent to live. The component takes a consent object — basis, who recorded it, when, and whether it covers every participant — and refuses the armed → recording edge without it. What it does not do is decide what a lawful basis is. It renders a state the host asserts.

The jurisdictional problem is real and is not ours to solve: around a dozen US states require all-party consent to record a conversation, several countries treat a clinical recording as special-category data, and a behavioural-health session may be a 42 CFR Part 2 record with disclosure rules of its own. The component's job is to make the asserted basis visible and attributable, and to make its absence block the transition. None of this copy has had legal review — see §18.

Disclosure follows the recording, not the surface

A sensitivity of restricted changes the component's behaviour, not just its badge: no third-party transcription, on-premise recogniser only, shorter retention, and the Strip art refuses to mount at all. A control that quiet cannot be the place a Part 2 recording begins. This is the same rule the context-menu brief settled — the surface cannot out-disclose its subject — pointed the other way.

Striking the record

Mid-consultation, a patient says something and immediately asks for it not to be recorded. Every ambient scribe on the market answers this with “stop and start again”, which loses the consultation's continuity and is exactly the moment a clinician will not be operating a UI.

Strike the last 30 seconds is one control, always in the same place, and it does three things: zeroes those samples in the buffer, writes a redaction record with who struck it and when, and leaves a visible hatched gap on the timeline. The gap matters. A removal a reader cannot see is a removal nobody can audit, and an audio file with an invisible splice is worse evidence than one with a labelled hole.

recorder-core: no React, no DOM, no runtime dependencies. The same shape as timeline-core, menu-core and signature-core, for the same reason.

A naming trap this repo has already hit

The npm barrel is flat, so every core's helpers collide on the obvious name. describeElapsed was taken and became describeElapsedShort; the same will happen to formatDuration, statusWord and fromObservation here. test/react-barrel.test.ts catches it, but it is cheaper to name them describeSilence, recorderStatusWord and so on from the start.

What ships where

  • packages/recorder-core — the engine above. Zero dependencies, per ADR 0009 and ENGINEERING.md §2.5.
  • registry/oxygen/recorder/ — the React component plus lib/recorder.tsx and lib/recorder.css, installed with oxygen add recorder.
  • packages/loaders-style custom elements — not in wave one. The loaders justify a framework-free build because a loading spinner is imported by non-React hosts; a recorder is wired to application state and will be used from React first. Revisit if a Vue or Angular customer asks.

Every one of the seven per-component gates applies unchanged: a recorder-core support item in the registry emitter and SUPPORT_ITEM_NAMES; path aliases in both the generator and apps/docs/tsconfig.json; the stylesheet in the React package copy list, the CSS-namespace owner test and the docs globals.css; component tokens declared before use; preview scenarios and card art, both required by docs-coverage.test.ts; and reciprocal related edges on both ends. Missing any one of them fails pnpm gen --strict, not review.

The position, and it is the menu brief's position again

Take the model, not the machinery. Section 17

Build plan

Four phases. The first produces something demonstrable in about a week; the fourth is where the money is and it cannot be started until a clinician and a lawyer have been found.

1

Engine and two arts — ~1 week

createSignal, PeakBuffer, the machine, and Bars + Strip. Stops at held — no upload, no recogniser. That is enough to demo the §01 claim, and the demo is the sales asset.

2

The fault catalogue — ~1 week

All thirteen of §11, each with a test that simulates it. This is the phase that is boring, unglamorous and the actual product. Do not let it slip behind the arts.

3

Playback, transcript, and the remaining arts — ~1.5 weeks

Pulse, Duet, Stream, the peaks sidecar contract, and the Stack companion. Duet is blocked on the platform emitting peaks at ingest — that is someone else's ticket and it should be raised now.

4

Consent, disclosure, redaction — blocked

The Console composition, the strike control, the Part 2 behaviour. Blocked on a named clinical reviewer and a legal review of the consent copy, and neither exists. Building the UI first and writing the copy later is how a component ships a confident sentence about lawful basis that nobody qualified has read.

Sequencing risk

Phase 4 is the differentiator and it is the blocked one. If the reviewer question stays unanswered, the honest thing is to ship phases 1–3 with consent as a host-supplied opaque object that the component renders and never authors — no copy, no defaults, no guidance — and say plainly in the docs that consent UX is out of scope. That is a worse product and a defensible one. The reverse is not.

Section 18

Eight questions I could not answer for you

Six are decisions. Two are the same unanswered questions from earlier briefs, now blocking a third and fourth time.

01
Do we ship the capture pipeline, or only the surface?
My recommendation: surface plus engine, not transport. The component renders a disposition it is told; it does not upload, retry or talk to a recogniser. Owning transport means owning auth, resumability and a queue, which is a product, not a component. The cost is that held and uploading need host wiring to mean anything.
02
Which recogniser contract do we target?
Bring-your-own with a typed adapter interface, or a reference adapter for one named vendor? A reference adapter makes the demo real and makes us look partisan. I lean bring-your-own with one worked example in the docs, but this is a business call.

Is on-device transcription in scope?
Whisper via WebGPU is now genuinely viable for short clips, and it is the only honest answer for a Part 2 record on a platform we do not control. §05. Turns entirely on whether the first paying customer is mobile/patient-facing or an ambient-scribe vendor on desktop. I chose Pulse on the reasoning that a level meter that survives at 44 px is harder to replace than a layout.

It was not on that list. On the brief's own effort×value axes it would rank in the top ten — it is closer to the money than most of group A. Adding it means something else moves out, and I have not picked what.

Section 19

What this does not do

Stated plainly, because a component that touches consent and clinical records will be read as claiming more than it does.

  • It does not obtain consent. It renders a basis the host asserts and refuses to start without one. It does not know what a lawful basis is in your jurisdiction, and the copy in §12 has had no legal review.
  • It does not move bytes. No upload, no retry, no queue, no recogniser. It renders where a recording is; the host is responsible for getting it there.
  • It does not guarantee capture. It detects and reports thirteen failure modes. There are more, and row 7 has no signal-level defence at all.
  • It is not an audit log. It emits ox-recorder-strike with who and when; persisting that, immutably, is the application's obligation.
  • It is not a medical device and not clinical decision support. The Rhythm art is held partly for this reason, and if it ever ships it must never appear beside a real rhythm strip.
  • It does not make anything compliant. HIPAA, GDPR, DPDP and 42 CFR Part 2 are properties of a system, not of a component. Oxygen UI is not a compliance boundary.

§01 and §11 row 7. Everything else here is craft. Those two are the reason a healthcare product should buy this rather than write it, and they are the two a competitor will not think to copy until a customer loses a consultation.
