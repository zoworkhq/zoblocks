ClinicalNote

A rich text editor is a solved problem. A clinical note is not. The gap between them is where this component lives — and the interesting engineering is not in bold and italic, it is in knowing who wrote every character and refusing to let anyone sign what they have not read.

01 — Thesis

A clinical note is not a document. It is a statement someone is accountable for.

Every editor decision follows from that sentence, and most editors get it wrong because they start from Google Docs instead.

Here is the thing nobody builds, and it is the reason this component is worth building at all.

Every character in a clinical note has an origin. Some of it the clinician typed. Some was dictated and transcribed with errors. Some came from a template that fired six paragraphs of normal-exam boilerplate. Some was pulled from structured data — a lab value, a medication list — and was true at the moment it was pulled and may not be true now. Some was copied forward from a note written three weeks ago by a different person about a different admission. And increasingly, some of it was written by a model.

In every EHR shipping today, all of that collapses into identical black text the moment it lands on screen. The clinician then signs the whole thing, and in doing so attests to it equally — the sentence they wrote and the sentence a machine wrote and the sentence that was true in March.

Three well-documented problems fall out of that single missing data structure:

ProblemWhat is measuredRoot cause
Note bloat / copy-forward A 2022 analysis of over 100 million notes found 50.1% of all note text duplicated from prior documentation on the same patient. In one UCSF study of 23,630 progress notes, 18% was manually entered, 46% copied, 36% imported. Copied text is indistinguishable from written text, so nobody can see the ratio — including the author.
Copy-paste error Copy-and-paste has been implicated in roughly a third of errors in ambulatory patient-safety analyses. Stale text has no expiry, no source link, and no visual weight difference from fresh text.
AI attribution CMS's July 2025 revision of its Medicare signature guidance treats an AI scribe exactly like a human scribe: the clinician signs, and the clinician is accountable. The tool need not even be named in the record. If AI text is visually identical to typed text, "the clinician reviewed it" is an unverifiable claim — including to the clinician.

Same missing structure, three times. Fix the structure and all three become addressable at once — not solved, but visible, which is the prerequisite.

The thesis in one line

Provenance is a property of text, not of the document. Store it per range, render it on demand, gate the signature on it, and serialize it alongside the note. Everything else in this brief is downstream of that decision.

The second load-bearing idea is smaller and just as consequential:

Corollary

A signed note is append-only. "Save" is the wrong verb after signature. The only legal operation on a signed clinical record is an addendum, and an editor whose primary button mutates the document in place is modelling the wrong object. This is not a preference; it is what makes the record admissible.

Why this is technically hard, stated honestly

Any competent developer can wire a toolbar to document.execCommand. The parts of this that are genuinely difficult, in rough order:

  1. Provenance marks that survive every edit. Type inside AI text and the range splits into three. Delete across a boundary and two marks merge. Paste into the middle of a pulled lab value and you need a fourth. Position mapping through arbitrary transforms is the whole problem, and it is exactly what ProseMirror's mark + step model already solves — which is most of the engine argument in §04.
  2. Deterministic serialization. The same document must produce the same bytes every time, or you cannot hash it, cannot detach a signature over it, and cannot prove a stored note was not altered. That means a hand-written serializer with stable attribute ordering, not DOMSerializer plus innerHTML.
  3. Structure the schema guarantees. A note that cannot be expressed as a valid Composition should be impossible to construct, not rejected at save time. Schema-first editors give this; schema-less ones do not.
  4. Offline that is not a second code path. A clinician on a ward with no signal must not lose a note. The honest solution is that the audit log and the offline queue are the same list of steps, replayed. Two mechanisms means two bugs.
  5. An editor that a screen reader can actually use. contenteditable is the most hostile surface in the platform for assistive technology, and no toolbar of icon buttons fixes it. §07.
02 — The reference, read closely

What the supplied "Scribble" design gets right, and the fourteen things it cannot survive

The mockup is a good general-purpose editor. Read as a clinical document surface, it has a specific set of failures — and the most serious one is in the footer, in four characters.

What it gets right, and should be kept

  • Sections as the visual unit. Chief Complaint, HPI, PMH, ROS as bold headings with body beneath is the correct mental model. It should become the actual model, not just the look of one.
  • The ink toggle. The T/pen pair in the toolbar is the most interesting thing in the design and the most under-used. Oxygen already ships a stroke engine for it.
  • A modal, not an inline field. Documentation deserves the whole screen. Correct.
  • Restraint. Twelve toolbar controls, not forty. The instinct is right even where the specific twelve are wrong.
  • A text-scale control. Shared workstations at a nursing station are used by people of every age and eyesight. Keep it — but see #9.

The fourteen

  1. 1
    "Scribble" is the wrong name for a medical record. A scribble is illegible, careless writing — the single most-litigated criticism of paper charts for fifty years. It is also a poor component name in a catalog: it does not say what it does. Call the component ClinicalNote. Keep "Scribble" as a product nickname if you like it, but not on the tin.
  2. 2
    B / I / U / S do almost nothing here, and underline is actively harmful. Underlined text in a clinical viewer reads as a hyperlink. Worse: this formatting does not survive the transports. FHIR narrative permits a restricted XHTML subset; C-CDA is stricter still; and HL7 v2 OBX — still how a great many notes actually move — is plain text. Bold survives, italic mostly survives, strikethrough silently vanishes in some renderers, which is catastrophic for the one case where it means something ("rule out MI"). Ship bold and italic, drop underline and strikethrough, and give the note a real retraction affordance instead.
  3. 3
    The image button, as drawn, is a PHI incident. A wound photo dropped into a note becomes a base64 blob inside the narrative — outside the imaging pipeline, with no consent record, no de-identification, no Media resource, no retention policy, and it will be silently stripped by any conforming FHIR narrative validator. Images belong as a reference to a stored Media/DocumentReference, uploaded through the host's pipeline, never inlined.
  4. 4
    The sparkle has no accept, no reject, no diff, and no attribution. This is the highest-liability control on the screen and it is a 24-pixel icon with no state. Under CMS's guidance the signer owns every word the model produced. An AI action that writes directly into the document, indistinguishable from typed text, makes "I reviewed it" unfalsifiable.
  5. 5
    "Last updated today, 14:38 AM" is invalid, and this is the one that would end up in a deposition. Three defects in six words: a 24-hour clock with an AM suffix is not a time; "today" is meaningless to anyone reading the record later; and there is no timezone or offset, so a note written at 23:50 in one zone lands on the wrong calendar day in another. 42 CFR 482.24(c)(1) requires record entries to be dated, timed, and authenticated. Render 16 Aug 2026, 14:38 IST (UTC+05:30) and put the relative form in a tooltip, never the reverse.
  6. 6
    "Save" is the wrong primary action, and "Cancel" is a trap. Clinical documentation has three distinct commits — save draft, sign, and addend — with different legal consequences and different reversibility. Collapsing them into one blue button means either the note is legally signed by an autosave, or a signed note is silently mutated. And "Cancel" next to twenty minutes of typing, with no destructive-action confirmation, will destroy someone's work this week.
  7. 7
    No author, no co-signer, no role. The note has no visible attribution. In teaching settings this is the norm rather than the exception: a resident writes, an attending countersigns with an attestation; a scribe documents, the physician attests to presence and accuracy. Neither is expressible here.
  8. 8
    Sections are bold text, not structure. "Chief Complaints" is styled to look like a heading. Nothing knows it is one — so nothing can validate that an H&P has an exam, nothing can map it to LOINC 10154-3, nothing can jump to it, and the C-CDA export has to guess by regex. Also: it is Chief Complaint, singular. The plural is a small tell, and clinicians notice small tells.
  9. 9
    "100%" is a text-scale control mislabelled as zoom, which is fine — once it is honest. Browser zoom already exists and users know where it is. What this control is actually good for is per-user text scale that persists across shared workstations. Label it, persist it to the user profile rather than the session, and make sure it changes the document and not the chrome.
  10. 10
    No autosave state, no draft recovery. Clinicians are interrupted mid-sentence as a matter of routine. Between the pager, the workstation timeout, and the browser tab that gets closed by the next person at the terminal, a note with no visible save state and no local persistence will lose work. This is the number-one complaint about every documentation tool ever shipped.
  11. 11
    No dot phrases, no smart links, no wildcards. The single highest-value feature in clinical documentation is text expansion, and it is absent. Every US clinician has muscle memory for .-triggered phrases; the omission is the difference between a 90-second note and a nine-minute one.
  12. 12
    Browser spellcheck will underline every drug name in the document. Out of the box, dyspnea, hematochezia, metformin and epistaxis are all "misspelled". Clinicians respond by turning spellcheck off entirely, which is how hyponatremia becomes hypernatremia in a signed note. This needs a medical dictionary, not a toggle.
  13. 13
    The body text is set at roughly 17px with a ~1900px measure. At that line length the eye loses its place returning to the start of a line, and clinical text is scanned rather than read. Cap the measure at ~80 characters and let the rest breathe.
  14. 14
    Nothing in this screen knows which patient it is about. There is no patient banner. Wrong-patient documentation is a named, tracked safety event, and the standard mitigation is a persistent identity banner in the same viewport as the entry surface — plus a check on paste, because pasted text from another chart is the most common way it happens.
The pattern behind all fourteen

Every defect is the same category error: the design treats a clinical note as a document when it is a record of an event. Documents get saved, edited, and formatted. Records get authored, attested, timestamped, amended, and — years later — read aloud in a room where the author has to explain them.

03 — Nine things that happen

What actually happens when a clinician writes a note

Feature lists come from scenarios, not from competitor screenshots. These nine are the ones that break naive editors.

Scenario 1

The interruption

Twelve minutes into an H&P, a code is called. The clinician walks away. The workstation locks after four minutes. Someone else badges in.

Requires: local durable draft keyed to note + author, survives lock, tab close, browser crash and power loss; explicit recovery on return; and the draft must not be visible to the next person at that terminal.

Scenario 2

The copy-forward

Day 4 of an admission. The clinician copies yesterday's assessment and edits two sentences. Eighty percent of today's note is a description of yesterday.

Requires: paste-source detection, per-range copy provenance, a visible ratio before signing, and — the useful part — a way to see only what changed.

Scenario 3

The AI draft

An ambient scribe produces a full note from the visit audio. It is 90% right. The 10% includes a medication the patient stopped taking in March.

Requires: AI text visually distinct and marked unreviewed; sign blocked until every AI range has been read or accepted; provenance recorded so the review can be evidenced later.

Scenario 4

The countersignature

A resident writes the note at 02:00. The attending reviews it at 08:00, adds a three-line attestation, and signs. Both signatures matter, in order, with times.

Requires: multi-party signature state, pending-cosign as a first-class value, attestation blocks that are attributable to the second author, and no silent mutation of the first author's text.

Scenario 5

The amendment

Three days after signing, a pathology result contradicts the documented assessment. The note must be corrected.

Requires: the original is immutable and stays readable; the correction is an appended, separately-signed, separately-timed addendum; both versions render together, in order, forever.

Scenario 6

The patient reads it

Under the Cures Act information-blocking rules, notes are released to the patient portal — often before the patient's next appointment, sometimes within minutes.

Requires: the author knows this while writing, not after; reading-level and stigmatizing-language hints; a sensitive-content path that is a deliberate, logged decision rather than a habit.

Scenario 7

The dictation

The clinician dictates. "Patient denies chest pain" is transcribed as "patient denies test pain". It is one character from clinically wrong and the clinician reads what they meant to say.

Requires: dictated ranges marked distinctly, low-confidence spans flagged by the recognizer surfaced as such, and undo that treats a dictated utterance as one unit rather than 200 keystrokes.

Scenario 8

The wrong chart

Two charts open in two tabs. Text is copied from one, pasted into the other. Different patient.

Requires: clipboard payloads carry their source patient identifier; paste into a different subject warns loudly and requires confirmation; the event is logged whichever way the clinician answers.

Scenario 9

The template that fired

A normal-exam macro inserts eleven systems of boilerplate. The patient has an obviously abnormal abdominal exam. The boilerplate says otherwise, and it is now signed.

Requires: template ranges marked as untouched-boilerplate; unfilled *** wildcards block signing; and the sign gate is honest that "you inserted 400 words and edited none of them" is worth saying out loud.

Observe

Seven of the nine are solved by the same two mechanisms: provenance marks and a sign gate. That is a strong signal that they are the right primitives — and that most of the rest of the feature list is decoration on top of them.

04 — Which engine

You asked about Quill. Quill is the wrong answer, and its data model is the right one.

Five real candidates, scored against what this component actually has to do rather than against a generic feature matrix.

The five requirements that decide it

  1. Attributed ranges that survive arbitrary edits — the provenance model.
  2. A validated schema — invalid notes must be unconstructable, not merely rejected.
  3. A DOM-free core — so the schema, serializers, lint rules and FHIR mapping are unit-tested in Node in milliseconds, exactly as signature-core is.
  4. An invertible, serializable edit log — because the audit trail, undo, offline sync and collaborative merge are all the same list.
  5. MIT, no vendor, no cloud tier — this is a component library for regulated buyers; a hosted dependency in the critical path is a procurement conversation you do not want to have.

Why Quill loses, in detail — because your instinct about it was half right

Quill's Delta format is genuinely the best idea in this space. A document is a list of insert operations, each carrying an attribute map. That is exactly the provenance model, expressed natively, with no invention required: 

// A Delta already says "this range has these properties".
{ ops: [
  { insert: "Patient reports ", attributes: { src: "typed" } },
  { insert: "progressive fatigue", attributes: { src: "dictated", conf: 0.71 } },
  { insert: " and exertional dyspnea.", attributes: { src: "ai", reviewed: false } },
] }

So the data model is right. Four things sink it anyway:

  • No schema. Quill has no way to state "a note is a sequence of coded sections; a section contains blocks; a signature block may not appear inside a section". Parchment blots describe rendering, not validity. Without a schema, every invariant becomes a runtime check you wrote and will forget to write next time — and the FHIR narrative constraint becomes a post-hoc validation that fails after the clinician has typed for twenty minutes.
  • Weak block structure. Deltas are flat. Nesting — sections containing sub-sections containing tables — is expressed by attribute conventions, and every consumer must re-derive the tree. For a document whose entire value proposition is structure, that is backwards.
  • Extension cost. Custom nodes (a live vitals table, an ink block, a wildcard placeholder) mean writing Blots against a 2013-era imperative API with a React wrapper bolted on. Every one of those is a day in ProseMirror and a week in Quill.
  • Trajectory. Quill 2.x is maintained — 2.4.5 shipped as a build-only maintenance release in mid-2026 — but it is a maintenance choice, not a growth one, and it is no longer the default recommendation for a greenfield React editor.
Steal this

Keep the Delta idea and drop the library. ProseMirror marks give the same attributed-range semantics plus a schema, and ReplaceStep gives the same op log plus invertibility. You lose nothing you wanted from Quill.

Why not Tiptap, given that everyone uses Tiptap

Tiptap is the right default for a CMS. It is the wrong dependency for this, for one structural reason: the three features Tiptap adds on top of ProseMirror that we would actually want — comments, document history, and AI — are precisely the ones behind the paid Cloud platform. So we would take the vendor dependency and still build those ourselves, because our versions have to be different anyway: our "comments" are clinical queries with a resolution workflow, our "history" is a legal addendum chain, and our "AI" is a review gate rather than a generator.

Tiptap's DX advantage is real and worth roughly two weeks. It is not worth a third-party pricing page in the middle of a healthcare procurement review. If Tiptap open-sources the collaboration stack, revisit it — this is a reversible decision, because a Tiptap extension is a thin wrapper over the same ProseMirror plugin we would write anyway.

Why not Lexical, which is the closest call

Lexical is faster, has the best accessibility investment of the five, and is MIT with no commercial tier. It loses on two things and they are the two that matter here: there is no schema, so document validity is a convention rather than a guarantee; and its editor state is snapshot-shaped rather than step-shaped, which means the audit log, the offline queue and the addendum chain each become separate machinery instead of three readings of one list.

If a future Oxygen decision is "we need a mobile-native editor with the same document model", Lexical becomes the stronger choice, and the -core package split below is what makes that switch cost weeks rather than quarters.

Decision

ProseMirror directly, with no framework wrapper. The document model, schema, transforms, serializers, lint rules and FHIR mapping live in @oxygenui-design/clinical-note-core and run in Node with zero DOM. The view layer and the antd shell live in @oxygenui-design/clinical-note. Same split as signature / signature-core, for the same reason: the expensive half is portable.

Cost, stated plainly

ProseMirror is roughly three to five times the code of Quill for the same visible toolbar. That cost buys the schema guarantee, the step log, and no vendor. If this component were a comment box, Quill would be correct and this brief would be one page.

05 — Sixty-three features

Sixty-three things it could do, and the fourteen that make v1

You asked what to consider. Here is everything worth considering, honestly labelled. v1 ships first. v2 is real but later. host is the integrator's job and we provide the seam. no is a deliberate refusal.

On "backslash in editor"

You are describing a trigger-character command system — and in this domain there are four of them, not one, because clinicians already have muscle memory for three. Getting these right is the difference between a 90-second note and a nine-minute one, and it is the single highest-ROI feature in the list.

TriggerOpensWhy this character
/ Block insert menu — section, table, vitals, ink pad, wildcard, addendum The modern convention (Notion, Linear, Slack). Non-clinical staff already know it. Only fires at the start of an empty block, so a date like 7/8 never triggers it.
. Dot phrases — .ros, .normalexam, .dcinstructions The one that matters. Epic's SmartPhrase trigger, learned by essentially every US clinician. Fires only after whitespace and only when followed by a letter, so sentence-ending periods and 7.1 g/dL are untouched.
@ Smart links — pull live patient data: @age, @meds, @lastcbc, @allergies Distinguishes "insert boilerplate" from "insert a fact about this patient", which is a genuinely different operation with a genuinely different provenance mark and staleness clock.
*** + F2 Jumps to the next unfilled wildcard in the document. A hint is one token — ***severity***, not ***how bad*** — so that two blanks on one line stay two blanks SmartList muscle memory. The template inserts blanks; F2 walks them. Unfilled wildcards at sign time are a blocking error — this is how boilerplate stops being a lie.

07 — Accessibility

A toolbar of icon buttons is not an accessibility strategy

contenteditable is the most hostile surface the web platform offers to assistive technology. Most editors respond by adding aria-label to the buttons and declaring victory. Here is the actual list.

Start here

A clinician with low vision, RSI, or a tremor is not an edge case in a hospital — they are a colleague, and documentation is the task they cannot delegate. An editor they cannot use is an editor that removes them from clinical practice.

RequirementWhat it means hereWhy editors get it wrong
Toolbar as one tab stop
WAI-ARIA toolbar pattern		 role="toolbar", roving tabindex, arrow keys within, one Tab in and one out. Fifteen individually-focusable buttons means fifteen Tab presses between the document and the sign button. Every time.
Every action has a keyboard path
2.1.1 Level A		 Including section insert, wildcard traversal, provenance toggle, AI accept/reject, and moving between sections. Slash menus and hover affordances are mouse-shaped by default; the keyboard path is added last and tested never.
No keyboard trap
2.1.2 Level A		 Esc closes the palette but must not close the modal underneath it, and Tab inside the document must insert a tab in a list context and move focus everywhere else. This exact conflict — Tab for indent vs Tab for focus — is the classic editor trap. The resolution: Tab indents only inside a list item; Esc then Tab always escapes.
Formatting is announced
4.1.2 / 1.3.1		 Marks must be real semantic elements (<strong>, <em>), not styled spans, so a screen reader can convey them at all. Editors that render bold as <span style="font-weight:600"> make emphasis literally invisible to a screen-reader user.
Provenance is not colour
1.4.1 Level A		 Each origin has a hue and a distinct underline style and an accessible-name annotation reachable from the caret. A tinted-background approach fails colour-blind users, forced-colors mode, and print simultaneously.
Status changes reach AT
4.1.3 Level AA		 Save state, offline transitions, autosave failures and sign-gate results announce via aria-live="polite"; a failed save is assertive. A silent "saving…" spinner is a status change nobody hears. And a save failure nobody hears is lost work.
Landmark navigation
2.4.1		 F6 cycles patient banner → toolbar → section rail → document → footer. Sections are real headings, so heading navigation works. Only possible because sections are schema nodes. This is the accessibility dividend of feature #14.
Focus is always visible
2.4.7 / 2.4.11 AA		 Including the caret inside contenteditable at 400% zoom, and the focused item in the command palette. Custom carets and virtualised documents lose the native caret and never replace it.
Reflow to 320 CSS px
1.4.10 AA		 At 400% zoom the rail collapses to a menu, the toolbar wraps, and the document stays in one column with no horizontal scroll. Fixed-width editor chrome is the most common 1.4.10 failure on the web.
Target size
2.5.8 AA (2.2)		 24×24 minimum with adequate spacing — and clinical density mode is where this gets violated, so the density tokens have a floor. "Compact" modes routinely ship 20px targets. On a tablet at the bedside, with gloves on, that is unusable.
Dragging has an alternative
2.5.7 AA (2.2)		 Section reorder works with Alt+↑/↓. Drag is additive. Drag-only reorder is a 2.2 failure and excludes anyone with a tremor.
IME and Android composition Composition events must not be interrupted by autosave, lint decorations, or provenance mark application. Decorations apply on compositionend, never during. This is where Slate historically bled, and it is not optional in a product shipping to India or East Asia.
Reduced motion
2.3.3 AAA, cheap		 Palette open, section jump, and diff transitions all become instant. Nobody remembers the palette animation.
The antd gaps we already know about

From the Signature work: antd's Modal has no accessible name unless you pass title; its initial focus lands on an invisible sentinel div; Tabs drop aria-controls unless given an id; and the close button's aria-label is hardcoded English. All four bite this component too, all four are fixed in the wrapper, and all four need a regression test — because they are antd behaviours we are patching from outside and an antd upgrade can silently reintroduce them.

The API rule that matters most

Nothing clinical is hard-coded and nothing clinical has a default. No phrase library, no terminology, no abbreviation list, no attestation wording, and no clock ships in the package. Every one of those is a jurisdictional, organisational or licensing decision. A component that guesses at them is confidently wrong in a domain where confidently wrong is the failure mode that hurts people.

Two consequences of that rule worth stating, because they will come up in review:

  • The do-not-use list is the one exception, and only because it is a short, stable, published list of things that are always wrong. It ships as data the host can replace, defaults to on, and warns rather than blocks.
  • Empty defaults must fail loudly in development. A ClinicalNote with no now prop should throw in dev, not silently call Date.now(). That lesson is already learned in signature-core and is enforced there by a test that greps the source.
09 — Data contract

FHIR, and the constraint that decides the whole formatting menu

The narrative subset is not a detail to handle at export time. It determines which toolbar buttons may exist at all.

The constraint

FHIR narrative div content must be an XHTML fragment containing only the basic HTML formatting elements from chapters 7–11 (excluding §4 of chapter 9) and chapter 15 of HTML 4.0, plus <a> elements and images, with styling applied through inline style attributes. It must not contain head, body, external stylesheet references, deprecated elements, scripts, forms, base/link/xlink, frames, iframes, objects, or event attributes such as onClick — so that the narrative is self-contained and carries no active content.

Why this is good news

Design the schema to be a subset of what FHIR permits, and the export becomes a total function. No validation step, no lossy conversion, no "this note cannot be transmitted" error twenty minutes after it was written. The schema is the contract. This is the argument for a schema-first engine, made concrete — and it is why the formatting menu in §06A is four buttons rather than twelve.

10 — Law

What the rules actually require, and what they do not

Enough to design correctly. Not legal advice, and every one of these needs the customer's counsel for their jurisdiction and deployment.

RuleWhat it saysWhat the component does
42 CFR 482.24(c)(1)
Medicare CoP, medical records		 Record entries must be dated, timed, and authenticated by the person responsible for providing or evaluating the service. Absolute timestamps with an offset, never relative. Author identity and role are required props. The clock comes from the host, because a browser clock is not evidence.
CMS signature guidance
revised July 2025 to cover AI		 Where a scribe is used — including artificial intelligence technology — the treating clinician signs the entry to authenticate the documentation and the care provided. Identifying who or what transcribed is not required. Two consequences. First: attribution is ours to offer, not a mandate — so provenance is a safety and review feature, not a compliance checkbox, and we should sell it as such. Second: since the signer owns every word regardless, the review gate is the thing that actually protects them.
21 CFR Part 11
where it applies — research, trials, regulated manufacturing		 Electronic signatures need two identification components, signature manifestations showing printed name, date, time and meaning, and audit trails that are computer-generated, time-stamped and do not obscure prior entries. The signature manifestation block in §06D is exactly that. Audit trails are emitted as steps; storage is the host's. Two-component authentication lives in the host's auth layer, never in a UI package.
21st Century Cures Act
information blocking		 Clinical notes must be released to patients without delay, with narrow exceptions. The author is told, in the editor, that the patient will read this. Reading-level and language hints are advisory. A sensitive-content path exists but is a deliberate, logged decision.
HIPAA 45 CFR 164.526
right to amend		 A patient may request amendment of their record. A granted amendment is appended and linked; the original is retained. Falls out of the addendum model for free. This is a second, independent reason the append-only design is correct.
Joint Commission "Do Not Use"
accreditation standard, not statute		 U, IU, QD, QOD, MS, MSO4, MgSO4, trailing zeros and missing leading zeros are prohibited in handwritten and free-text entries. Trailing zeros remain permissible where precision genuinely requires them — lab results, lesion sizes, catheter sizes. Lint, warn, offer the expansion. Never block: it is a standard, not a law, and that trailing-zero carve-out guarantees false positives in exactly the text clinicians write most.
MDR / FDA device rules Software that interprets clinical data to inform a diagnosis or treatment decision may be a regulated medical device. Hard boundary. The component displays, structures and lints text; it never suggests a diagnosis, a dose, or a treatment. The CDS seam exists for hosts; nothing clinical ships through it from us.
DPDP Act 2023 · GDPR Health data is sensitive personal data with residency, purpose-limitation, and minimisation obligations. Nothing leaves the browser. The local draft store is opt-in, scoped to the author, and cleared on commit and on author change. No telemetry, ever — and this is worth saying in the README, because buyers ask.
The line, stated the way it must appear in the README

Oxygen UI provides user-interface components. This component does not make an application HIPAA, GDPR or DPDP compliant, is not a medical device, is not clinical decision support, and does not by itself establish the legal validity or admissibility of any record. That depends on the deployment, the jurisdiction, and the procedures around it.

11 — Architecture

Two packages, four layers, and three things not to build

L0@oxygenui-design/fhir · tokens · intl — types, tokens, locale. No React. Exists today.
L1@oxygenui-design/clinical-note-core — the engine. Schema, provenance marks, gate rules, expansion, canonical/narrative/text serializers, FHIR mapping. ProseMirror model + transform only — state turned out to be view-side and is not needed here. No view, no DOM, no React, no antd.
L1@oxygenui-design/signature-core — reused verbatim for ink strokes and the co-signature outcome union. Already shipped, already tested.
L2@oxygenui-design/clinical-note — the React view, the antd shell, the toolbar, the palettes, the gate modal, the reader. antd v6 as a peer dependency.
L3Blocks — a full documentation screen: chart context, prior notes, orders. Later, and only once someone asks.

The split is the same one signature / signature-core made, for the same reason and with the same payoff: the expensive half is portable and testable without a browser. If the library-wide antd decision reverses, or if a React Native target appears, or if Lexical becomes the better view layer in 2028, none of that reaches the engine.

Three things not to build

Do not build

A speech recognizer

Months of work, a hard vendor market, and the wrong core competency. Build the channel — a typed API for transcript segments with confidence — and let Deepgram, Nuance or the platform's own recognizer plug into it. The channel is the defensible part anyway, because it is what carries provenance.

Do not build

A collaboration server

ProseMirror's collab module needs an authority. That is infrastructure, not a component, and shipping it would make Oxygen a hosting company. Document the integration, ship an example against a plain WebSocket authority, and stop there.

Do not build

Terminology or content

SNOMED, ICD-10 and RxNorm carry licensing we cannot redistribute, and a phrase library is an organisational asset with clinical governance behind it. We own the popup, the keyboard model, and the inserted node. They own the words.

Bundle budget

ProseMirror is not small, and pretending otherwise in a library that enforces size-limit in CI would be dishonest. Rough shape, to be asserted rather than estimated once real:

Entry pointContainsBudget posture
clinical-note/readerRead-only render + print. No editor, no ProseMirror view.Tight. Most people who open a note never edit one, and this is the path that must stay cheap.
clinical-note-coreSchema, gate, serializers. No view.Moderate — and it is the piece a server also imports, so it is worth keeping honest.
clinical-noteThe full editor.Generous, and lazily loaded by the host. An editor is not on the critical rendering path of any chart view.

The rule: the reader must not pull the editor. If that separation is not enforced by a size budget in CI, it will be violated by the third import someone adds.

12 — Testing

How to test an editor without a flaky test suite

The general rule: if it needs a real browser to assert, it is in the wrong package.

LayerHowSpeed
Schema invariants Property-based. Generate random documents from the schema; assert every one serializes to valid FHIR narrative and survives a round trip. This is the test that makes the schema-first argument pay. Node, milliseconds
Provenance under transforms Fuzz: apply thousands of random steps to a marked document and assert marks never lose coverage, never overlap incorrectly, and never leave orphaned zero-length ranges. This is where the bugs will be, so this is where the fuzzer goes. Node, milliseconds
Serializer determinism Golden files, plus: serialize twice from independently-built equal documents and diff the bytes. Attribute ordering bugs only appear this way. Node, milliseconds
Gate rules Table-driven. Each rule is a pure function of (document, context) → verdict. No editor needed to test any of them. Node, milliseconds
Expansion Golden: phrase in, document fragment out. Includes wildcard counting and nested pick lists. Node, milliseconds
Keyboard and palette Testing Library, real key events, no fireEvent.change shortcuts. Assert the roving-tabindex contract explicitly — one Tab in, one Tab out. jsdom, fast
IME / composition Synthetic compositionstart/update/end sequences, asserting no decoration is applied mid-composition. Then a real Playwright run on an actual IME, because jsdom will lie to you here. jsdom + one real browser
Accessibility axe per story, zero violations, enforced by the existing coverage manifest. Plus one manual NVDA and one VoiceOver pass per release — axe cannot tell you whether an editor is usable, only that it is not obviously broken. CI + human
Visual Playwright VRT on the existing @vrt tag: light, dark, forced-colors, three densities, 400% zoom, 320px width, and the print stylesheet. CI
Offline and crash Playwright: kill the context mid-edit, reopen, assert the draft recovers with the right content and the right author. Then assert it does not recover for a different author. CI
No-clock, no-DOM discipline A test that greps clinical-note-core for Date.now, new Date(, document. and window. and fails on a hit. Crude, and it has already earned its place in signature-core. Node, instant
The number that matters

If the core test suite does not run in under a second, the fuzz tests will be deleted within a month and the provenance bugs will ship. Keeping the engine DOM-free is not architectural purity — it is what makes the tests fast enough that people keep running them.

14 — Your call

Three decisions before any code is written

Each one changes the package layout, so each one is cheap now and expensive in week four.

Decision 1

The name

Recommendation: ClinicalNote, package @oxygenui-design/clinical-note, registry item clinical-note.

It sits in the family the repo already has — ClinicalValue, ReferenceRange — and it is what a buyer searches for. "Scribble" is a fine product nickname and a poor component name: a scribble is illegible writing, which is the oldest criticism of medical records there is.

Rejected: Scribe (now means ambient AI, collides with the vendor category), Chart (collides with pro-charts), Note (unsearchable at catalog scale).

Decision 2

How far antd goes

Signature was the pilot for wrapping antd. This component is much bigger and would make the antd dependency structural rather than experimental.

Recommendation: wrap antd for the shell only — Modal, Button, Tabs, Alert, Tag, Dropdown — and keep the editor surface, palette and provenance layer as plain elements with tokens. That keeps the antd surface small enough that a future registry-channel version is a shell swap rather than a rewrite.

The open question from the Signature work stands: if the shadcn registry channel is to survive, the wrap/match/migrate call has to be made before the second big antd component. This is the second one.

Decision 3

Free or Pro

Recommendation: tier: "pro", with Reader free.

This is the most valuable component in the catalog and the most expensive to build. The read-only renderer being free means notes produced by a Pro editor can be displayed anywhere, which is good for adoption and costs nothing — the editor is the asset.

It also fits the services-and-contracts revenue model: this is the component a health-tech buyer books a call about rather than swiping a card for.

And one thing I would push back on, if you disagree

If the reaction to this brief is "that is a lot — can we just ship the reference design on Quill in a week": yes, and it would be a mistake worth naming. That version competes with every rich text editor on npm on price and polish, and it loses. The version in this brief competes with nothing, because provenance-marked clinical text with a composable sign gate is not a product anyone else sells as a component.

The middle path, if seven weeks is too long: build phases 0–3 and 5 — schema, provenance, serializers, the shell, and the gate — and ship without the trigger characters. That is four weeks, it is still differentiated, and phase 4 slots in afterwards without rework because the palette is additive. What should not be cut is phase 1, because it is the phase that proves the idea works at all.
