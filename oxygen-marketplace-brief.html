01

The console is the only screen Oxygen owns where a customer is already signed in — and already spending.

The console today does one job: an organisation authors a design language, validates it against the same gate the build uses, and publishes it as an immutable stylesheet at /t/{org}/{theme}@{version}.css. Themes, fonts, icon slots, brand artwork, four roles, an append-only audit log, and org scoping enforced by construction rather than by convention.

Every one of those pieces is already half of a marketplace. The icon slots are a delivery target. The content-addressed asset store is a CDN. The immutable version pointer is a licence boundary. The audit log is a receipt. What is missing is the part that says this organisation is entitled to this thing, because it paid for it.

The honest objection, first

The feasibility review already threw out one revenue model on TAM grounds: a $199 lifetime tier priced against Magic UI, whose comparable converts against every React developer alive, while the healthcare-FHIR frontend population is two orders of magnitude smaller. A marketplace of $12 icon packs fails that same arithmetic harder — smaller unit, same audience, plus a content-production cost the component library does not have.

So the marketplace has to earn its place on something other than unit sales. It does, on four counts:

  1. It is a delivery mechanism the enterprise motion already needs. When a $25k contract includes a private component pack, something has to hand the customer the bytes, prove they are entitled to them, and revoke them when the contract ends. Today that is an email with a zip. That is the actual gap.
  2. It converts a designer into a lead without procurement. A design lead can put $290 on a card. They cannot put $25,000 on one. The card purchase is the qualification event: we now know the org, the stack, the framework, and that somebody there has budget authority.
  3. It gives the free MIT core somewhere to point. A library with no paid surface has to convert readers into contract conversations in one leap. A $180 pack is the intermediate step.
  4. The top SKUs are already contract-sized. An app starter at $4,000 and a commissioned component at $12,000 are not marketplace impulse buys; they are the bottom of the services ladder with a checkout button on them, which shortens a six-week sales cycle to a form.
The frame this brief argues for

Build entitlements and delivery first, storefront second. The storefront is the cheap part and the part that can be rebuilt. The entitlement record, the licence boundary, the revocation path and the audit trail are the parts that are painful to retrofit once real money has moved through them.

The v1 cut

Four kinds ship first — icons, illustrations, theme packs and pro components — because each reuses machinery the console already has: icon slots, the content-addressed asset store, the theme import path, and the shadcn registry. Fixtures are the fifth if the content is cheaper than expected, because packages/fixtures already exists.

And depth beats breadth at launch. Six categories with one pack each reads as abandoned. One category with six packs reads as a product line.

The flagship is an empty state, because "empty" is three different facts and every stock illustration says the wrong one

This is the row worth building the marketplace around, and the argument for it is the library's own founding argument. From the README: a blank cell is indistinguishable from a rendering bug.

What the pack contains, so it is a product and not a mood

  • 24 regions × 3 meanings = 72 illustrations, plus a "not applicable" variant where clinically real (menstrual history for a male patient recorded as not-applicable rather than empty).
  • The copy for each, written to be truthful rather than cheerful, with the reasoning recorded beside it.
  • A React component per meaning, not just SVG files — because the point is that the developer picks a meaning, and the artwork follows. <EmptyState reason="not-asked" /> makes the wrong version harder to ship than the right one.
  • Forced-colors and reduced-motion behaviour already resolved, since a line illustration that vanishes in high-contrast mode is a blank region again.
  • A mapping table from FHIR dataAbsentReason codes (asked-unknown, not-asked, masked, not-applicable) to the state to render, so the choice can be made from data rather than by hand.
Why this is the flagship rather than the icon set

The icon set is better than the competition. The empty-state system has no competition, because selling it requires a point of view about clinical data that an illustration marketplace has no reason to hold. It is also the cheapest possible demonstration of what the whole library claims, which makes it the best top-of-funnel asset Oxygen could put on a pricing page.

04

Five things not to sell, decided now rather than after a letter arrives

A marketplace makes it trivially easy to list something. These are listed here so that "we could add it later" is a decision somebody reverses deliberately.

1 · SNOMED CT content

SNOMED CT requires an affiliate licence and its terms are country-dependent; redistributing descriptions inside a $350 pack is not a grey area. Sell the mapping layer we author, never the terminology we do not own. LOINC and RxNorm are more permissive but still carry attribution requirements — which is exactly the kind of detail that belongs on the item's licence record rather than in somebody's memory.

2 · Redistributed font licences

The console stores uploaded font bytes, which is fine for a customer's own licensed face. Selling a bundle that includes a commercial face means becoming a font reseller, with per-seat web-licence accounting attached. Sell typography configurations over openly licensed families.

3 · Clinical alarm tones

Audible alarms in medical electrical equipment are the subject of IEC 60601-1-8. A "clinical alert sound pack" invites a customer to use it somewhere the standard applies, and the moment they do, our artwork is inside their conformity assessment. Say no, and say why on the page.

4 · Patient-education artwork, for now

An inhaler-technique diagram is instructions to a patient. If it is wrong, the harm is direct, and "it was decorative" is not a defence. This can ship later with a named clinical reviewer and an explicit scope, but it must not ship in the first release alongside decorative spot art under one licence.

5 · Anything that computes a clinical recommendation

A vitals flowsheet renders values. A component that says "score 7 — escalate" is clinical decision support, and depending on jurisdiction and claim, that can be a regulated medical device. The line for the marketplace: we render, compute arithmetic that the user configured, and never advise. The NEWS2-style scores get a fixture pack and a renderer, not a built-in escalation rule.

Also: disclose AI-assisted artwork

Procurement questionnaires increasingly ask. A disclosed "AI-assisted, human-finished, reviewed by X" is a fact a buyer can accept. An undisclosed one that gets noticed is a credibility event for a company whose entire pitch is the claims are checkable. Put the provenance on the item record — §5.

05

Every item carries a record of what was checked — the same move the theme validator already makes

A published theme version carries a ValidationRecord and a validatorVersion, and the delivery route refuses to serve a version whose validator has since been tightened. That is an unusual and good idea, and it generalises: a marketplace item should carry the evidence for its claims, and the storefront should render the evidence rather than an adjective.

"Accessible" is marketing. "17 of 17 foreground/background pairs at or above 4.5:1, forced-colors verified 14 Aug 2026 against the current checker" is a fact, and it is one the customer can hand to their own procurement team. Which means the record is not overhead — it is a second product hiding inside the first.

Why this is worth the effort

It makes the marketplace hard to copy and easy to price. Anybody can draw 320 glyphs. Producing 320 glyphs with a named clinician's review scope, a contrast record and an explicit list of things they do not claim requires a company that already thinks this way — which is the only durable advantage a small library has over a large one.

06

The storefront, in the console's own chrome — one new rail item, not a second product

The console's design rules are already written down and they constrain this screen more than a blank page would: the instrument is the lit object (anything rendering something sits in a dark panel), red only ever means critical (so a "Sale" badge cannot be red), and where a customer's own colours are on screen, the chrome goes achromatic. A marketplace card previewing a customer-themable icon pack is exactly that case.

The two rules that shape this flow

Nothing purchased ever reaches production by itself. An icon pack applied to a theme creates a draft; a human with theme.publish still has to publish it. The console already separates designer from admin precisely because publishing reaches a customer's production application, and a purchase must not become a back door around that split.

The purchase belongs to the organisation, not the buyer. A designer who leaves does not take the icon pack with them, and the entitlement is not attached to their member document. This is also why the receipt and the invoice live on the Purchases page rather than in one person's email.

The discovery problem, stated plainly

A storefront visible only after sign-up has no top of funnel. The catalogue's public pages should live on oxygenui.design, where the docs already rank, with the console owning checkout, entitlement and delivery. Same catalogue documents, two renderings. Building the storefront console-only would be building a shop inside a locked building.

09

One-time, per organisation, perpetual — and an invoice for the half of this market that cannot use a card

Why not per seat

Seat-counting artwork is unenforceable and insulting in equal measure. The customer would have to tell us how many designers opened an SVG, and we would have to pretend to check. Per-organisation is honest, it is what the console's data model already expresses, and it removes the entire class of "does the contractor count" support conversation.

Why not a subscription, yet

An all-access subscription is the obvious second move and the wrong first one. Subscriptions to a small audience produce churn management work before they produce revenue, and they force a content cadence — a promise of new packs every month — that a two-person content operation cannot keep. Ship one-time purchases; add "Oxygen Plus" only when the attach rate shows more than one purchase per org. Stripe's own Entitlements API is subscription-only, which is another reason our entitlement records have to be ours regardless.

The point of the fourth row

It is the reason to build this at all. Once entitlements exist as records rather than as emails, delivering the contract-sized work becomes the same operation as delivering a $180 pack — including revocation when a trial ends, which today has no mechanism whatsoever.

Tax, currency and the boring parts that bite later

  • Stripe Tax on, because digital goods are taxed where the customer is, and the thresholds arrive before the revenue does. Checkout collects the billing address that the calculation needs.
  • Prices in USD with Stripe handling presentment. One currency of record keeps reconciliation trivial while volume is low.
  • Business-customer field — VAT/GST number captured at checkout, because a B2B buyer who cannot enter theirs will open a support ticket instead of paying.
  • EU digital-content withdrawal right — for consumer-classified buyers, immediate access requires an explicit acknowledgement that the 14-day withdrawal right is waived on download. Collected at checkout, recorded on the order.
  • Refunds — a stated window (14 days, no questions, one per org per item), executed as an entitlement revocation plus a Stripe refund. §14 covers what revocation cannot undo.
10

Stripe, concretely — and the one line that keeps a double-delivered webhook from double-granting

Stripe's own guidance is unambiguous on two points, and both of them are design constraints rather than implementation details: webhooks are required — a customer can pay and lose their connection before the success page loads — and the fulfilment function must survive being called several times, possibly concurrently, for the same session.

The atomic claim — the same trick the codebase already uses for the first admin

claimFirstAdmin in hq makes "is this the first account" a single atomic write rather than a read followed by an act, because a count is a read and several simultaneous signups all read zero. Order fulfilment is exactly the same shape: two concurrent calls both read "not yet fulfilled" and both grant. So it is written the same way — and the order document's _id is the Stripe session id, which is the same reason the font store keys documents by their digest. 

// src/lib/market/fulfil.ts — safe to call many times, concurrently,
// from the webhook and from the success page, for the same session.
export async function fulfil(sessionId: string): Promise<Fulfilment> {
  const session = await stripe.checkout.sessions.retrieve(sessionId, {
    expand: ["line_items"],
  });
  if (session.payment_status === "unpaid") return { granted: [], reason: "unpaid" };

  // The claim. `_id` is the session id, so the upsert is the lock:
  // exactly one caller sees `fulfilledAt` transition from null.
  const claim = await orders().findOneAndUpdate(
    { _id: sessionId, fulfilledAt: null },
    {
      $setOnInsert: { orgId, memberId, items, amountTotal, currency, createdAt: now },
      $set: { fulfilledAt: now },
    },
    { upsert: true, returnDocument: "before" },
  );

  // A document came back with fulfilledAt already set → somebody else won.
  // Not an error: return what they granted.
  if (claim?.fulfilledAt) return { granted: claim.granted, reason: "already" };

  const granted = await grantEntitlements(orgId, items, { via: sessionId });
  await audit("market.purchased", { orgId, actorId: memberId, subject: items.join() });
  return { granted, reason: "granted" };
}
The vulnerability this shape closes, and one it does not

Never accept a price, amount or line item from the client. The browser sends an item slug; the server looks up the price. A checkout endpoint that takes { priceId } or, worse, { amount } is a marketplace that sells $450 components for one cent, and it is the single most common integration mistake in this category.

And what it does not close: the webhook endpoint must be exempt from any CSRF/session middleware, must read the raw body before any JSON parsing, and must return 2xx quickly — Checkout waits up to ten seconds on checkout.session.completed before redirecting the customer, so slow fulfilment is a visible stall.

Events to handle, and what each one means

Event Do
checkout.session.completed Fulfil. The common path.
checkout.session.async_payment_succeeded Fulfil. Bank debits land here, sometimes days later. Skipping it means silently never delivering to the customers who paid the most.
checkout.session.async_payment_failed Email the buyer. Do not grant.
checkout.session.expired Mark the order abandoned. Useful signal: a spike here is a pricing or tax-surprise problem, not a bug.
invoice.paid Grant for the enterprise path.
charge.refunded Revoke the entitlement, revoke registry tokens' access to that item, audit it.
charge.dispute.created Revoke and alert a human. A dispute on a digital good is usually either fraud or a very unhappy customer, and both need a person.

Local development uses stripe listen --forward-to localhost:6003/api/stripe/webhook. Two environment variables join the console's existing scripts/env.mjs convention — STRIPE_SECRET_KEY and STRIPE_WEBHOOK_SECRET — and the app must refuse to boot in production with test-mode keys, which is one line and prevents an embarrassing week.

11

Five collections, and the first document in this codebase that is deliberately not org-scoped

The console's central invariant is that everything belonging to a customer carries orgId, nothing reads a collection directly, and scoped() returns an object that cannot express an unscoped query. There are two documented exceptions today, both named unscoped*: sign-in, and the public stylesheet route.

The catalogue is the third, and it is a different kind: it is not customer data at all. Every organisation sees the same items. That has to be stated loudly, because a reviewer skimming catalogItems.find({}) should be able to tell in one second whether they are looking at the deliberate exception or the bug the whole scoping design exists to prevent — and the isolation test that asserts nothing under src/ imports db() directly will need an explicit, narrow allowance.

Collection Scoped? Key shape Notes
catalogItems global slug unique kind, title, blurb, stripePriceId, current version, provenance (§5), preview assets, framework compatibility. Read through unscopedCatalogue().
catalogVersions global { itemId, version } unique Immutable, exactly like themeVersions. A purchase entitles an org to a version line; an update writes a new document. "Which version did they buy, and which have they installed" stays answerable.
orders orgId _id = Stripe session or invoice id The id choice is the idempotency mechanism. Amount, currency, tax, buyer, line items, fulfilledAt, refund state.
entitlements orgId { orgId, itemId } unique grantedAt, grantedVia (order id, or contract:… with a reason and an actor), versionLine, revokedAt + revokedReason. Never deleted — the console does not delete referenced things.
registryTokens orgId _id = SHA-256 of the token Only the hash is stored, exactly like sessions and password resets. Label, createdBy, lastUsedAt, expiresAt (TTL index), revokedAt. Shown once at mint time and never again.

Marketplace bytes reuse the content-addressed pattern the font store already established — SHA-256 as _id, so a re-upload is idempotent and "are these the bytes we shipped" is answerable by recomputing rather than by trusting a filename. They are global rather than copied per organisation; what is per-organisation is the entitlement to fetch them.

Three audit actions join the existing union: market.purchased, market.installed, market.revoked. The audit log is append-only and already indexed by { orgId, at: -1 }, so the Purchases page's history view is a query rather than a feature.

12

Four capabilities, and spending the organisation's money is admin-only for the same reason publishing is

The console checks capabilities, not roles — can(role, "theme.publish") survives a fifth role; role === "admin" || role === "owner" scattered across twenty actions does not. Four capabilities join the union.

Capability admin designer developer viewer Reasoning
market.browse ✓ ✓ ✓ ✓ Seeing what exists costs nothing and creates demand. A viewer who spots a pack tells the admin.
market.purchase ✓ — — — Admin only. Same argument as publishing: it is the action with consequences outside the console. A designer requests; an admin approves by buying. Anything else means a contractor can spend a hospital's money on a Tuesday.
market.install ✓ ✓ — — Installing a pack edits a theme draft, so it is really theme.write wearing a different hat — and it stops at the draft, because publishing stays separate.

One deliberate asymmetry

A designer can spend an admin's money indirectly by asking, but cannot do it directly — while a developer can mint a credential that downloads everything the org owns. That is the right split: the first is a financial control, the second is a delivery convenience, and conflating them produces either a console nobody can use or a card nobody controls.

13

Delivery is different for every kind — and the existing public asset route is the one thing that must not be reused

The console serves /t/{org}/{theme}@{v}.css and /f/{org}/{sha}.{ext} publicly and without credentials, deliberately, because a browser fetches a stylesheet and a font with no session. That property is correct there and fatal here: a paid pack served from an unauthenticated URL is a paid pack that is one shared link away from being free.

So marketplace bytes get their own route, /m/{item}@{v}/{file}, which resolves an entitlement before it resolves a file — and a miss is a flat 404 either way, because "that item exists and you have not bought it" is not a fact this route needs to volunteer.

Kind Lands as Reuses Watch for
Icons Written straight into the theme's replaceable icon slots, as a draft. uploadIcons() — same slot model, same size ceiling, same SVG-only rule. Run checkLogo() on our own artwork too. "We drew it" is exactly the assumption that ships an SVG with a script in it.
Illustrations A zip download, plus an optional copy into the org's asset store so they get a stable /f/{org}/{sha} URL for use in the customer's own app. The content-addressed store, the digest-as-id trick. The copy is a deliberate duplication: once it is in the customer's store it is theirs and survives a refund. That is a licence question, not a technical one.
Components A private shadcn registry namespace the CLI installs from. The registry the public library already ships. Token handling — see below.
Themes A draft theme in the org's list, never published. The existing import/transfer path and its validator. A purchased theme that fails the current validator must be rejected at import with the reason, not stored broken.
Fixtures / data Download, and a registry item so it can be installed into a test directory. packages/fixtures. Nothing derived from a real patient. Ever, for any reason, including "it was anonymised".

Components: shadcn already solved the hard half

The shadcn CLI supports namespaced registries with per-registry authentication configured in components.json, expanding ${VAR} from the environment. So the customer's install experience for a paid component is one line, and no token is ever committed: 

// the customer's components.json
{
  "registries": {
    "@oxygen": "https://oxygenui.design/r/{name}.json",
    "@oxygen-pro": {
      "url": "https://console.oxygenui.design/r/pro/{name}.json",
      "headers": { "Authorization": "Bearer ${OXYGEN_TOKEN}" }
    }
  }
}
# .env.local, never committed. Minted in the console, shown once.
OXYGEN_TOKEN=oxy_live_…

npx shadcn@latest add @oxygen-pro/vitals-flowsheet

On our side, /r/pro/{name}.json does four things in order and refuses at the first failure:

  1. Hash the bearer token and look it up. Only the SHA-256 is stored, so a database dump yields nothing usable — the same reasoning as the session cookie.
  2. Resolve the organisation from the token, never from a parameter.
  3. Check the entitlement for this item, and that it is not revoked.
  4. Serve the registry JSON with the file contents inlined, stamp lastUsedAt, and rate-limit per token.

Everything else is a 404. A 403 would confirm the item's existence to somebody guessing names, and there is no reason to help.

An SVG is a document, and this origin has privileges

The console already knows this — checkLogo refuses executable artwork for exactly this reason. Marketplace files are bigger, more numerous and authored under deadline, so the same rule needs teeth at the route: Content-Disposition: attachment on downloads, X-Content-Type-Options: nosniff, a restrictive CSP on any inline preview, and — best of all — serve the bytes from a separate origin so a hostile SVG cannot reach a console session even if one gets through review.

14

Nine ways this goes wrong, and what each one is worth doing about

1 · The TAM arithmetic, again

$180 packs into a niche audience will not fund the company. Answer: judge the marketplace on entitlement-delivery for contracts and on qualified leads produced, not on pack revenue. If after two quarters it has not produced a single contract conversation, it is a hobby and should be frozen rather than expanded.

2 · Content production is the real cost

320 glyphs and 72 illustrations is months of skilled work, and the code is the cheap half. Answer: cost the content before building the storefront. Launch one category with depth. A catalogue of four lonely items reads as abandoned and damages the library it is meant to sell.

3 · Clinical review is recurring, not one-off

The review record is the differentiator, so it must be budgeted per item and re-run when an item changes. Answer: a named reviewer with a rate, a scope template, and a rule that an item whose review is older than its current version cannot be listed — the same relationship validatorVersion already enforces for themes.

4 · The catalogue is the first global collection

Every scoping mistake in this codebase would look like a normal query. Answer: unscopedCatalogue() named like the two existing exceptions, a narrowly-scoped allowance in the isolation test, and a test that asserts two organisations' entitlement queries never return each other's rows.

5 · Refunds cannot un-download bytes

Revocation stops future fetches; it does not reach a zip on a laptop. Answer: say so in the licence in plain words, keep the refund window short, limit one refund per org per item, and treat a pattern of buy-download-refund as fraud rather than as a policy gap.

6 · Licence contamination

The core is MIT. A marketplace next to it invites "will the free parts get worse?" and, worse, invites a paid asset to leak into an MIT package. Answer: publish the boundary — nothing already MIT is ever withdrawn — and add a build check that no registry item under the MIT licence references a marketplace asset path.

7 · Payment-integration classics

Client-supplied prices, unverified webhook signatures, non-idempotent fulfilment, ignoring async payment events. Answer: §10, plus tests that replay the same webhook twice and assert one entitlement, and a test that posts an unsigned body and asserts a 400.

8 · Tax registration sneaks up

Digital goods are taxed at the customer's location and thresholds are low in several places. Answer: Stripe Tax from the first sale, not the hundredth, and an accountant conversation before the first non-domestic invoice rather than after.

9 · The console is not on main

The whole app arrived in 8809f44 on staging — not on claude/multi-framework-ui-theming-52a8a8, which is stale at main — and is not an ancestor of main. Answer taken: the marketplace was built on top of staging, which was a clean fast-forward from main. Landing staging on main remains a prerequisite for release.

15

Six phases, and money does not appear until the third

P0

Merge the console

Done. The console lives on staging (8809f44), which is main plus one commit — so this was a fast-forward rather than a merge. The marketplace is built on top of it. Landing staging on main is still the release prerequisite, with the lockfile care that has twice turned main red.

P1

The spine, with no payment in it

catalogItems, catalogVersions, entitlements, the /m/ delivery route, the four capabilities, the three audit actions, and a free pack that goes end to end: browse → grant → install into a theme draft. Proves delivery before proving billing, and is independently useful for contract bundles.

P2

Stripe

orders, Checkout Session creation from a server-resolved price, the webhook with signature verification, the atomic fulfil(), the success page calling the same function, receipts, and the refund path. One paid item. Tests: replayed webhook, unsigned body, async payment succeeded, refund revokes.

P3

The storefront

Catalogue, item page rendering the provenance record, Purchases, install actions. Public catalogue pages mirrored onto oxygenui.design for discovery, because a shop inside a locked building has no customers.

P4

Registry entitlement

registryTokens, minting UI, /r/pro/{name}.json gated on token → org → entitlement, rate limiting, and the components.json documentation. This is what makes paid components possible rather than only paid assets.

P5

Enterprise and depth

Stripe Invoicing with PO numbers, multi-item purchase, contract-granted entitlements with a reason and an actor, and — in parallel and on a longer clock than all of the above — the content production line that gives the catalogue something worth browsing.

Sequencing claim worth arguing with

P1 before P2 is the load-bearing choice. It is tempting to put a Checkout button on a page in an afternoon and call it a marketplace, but then entitlements get invented under deadline in a webhook handler, and the record that has to survive refunds, contracts, audits and revocation is the one nobody designed.

Ten calls I made writing this, and seven that are yours

Made, with the reasoning above

# Decision Because
1 Entitlements and delivery before storefront and payment The retrofit cost is asymmetric, and contracts need it regardless of whether anyone buys a pack.
2 Per-organisation, perpetual, one-time Seat counting on artwork is unenforceable; subscriptions impose a content cadence we cannot yet keep.
3 market.purchase is admin-only Same argument the console already makes for publishing: the action with consequences outside the console gets the narrower grant.
4 Purchases never auto-publish A purchase must not be a back door around the designer/admin split.
5 A separate /m/ route rather than reusing /f/ /f/ is public by design and correct; paid bytes need an entitlement check before a file lookup.
6 Order _id = Stripe session id Makes idempotent fulfilment structural rather than remembered — the codebase's existing idiom for digests and atomic claims.
7 The empty-state system is the flagship It is the only item on the list with no substitute, and it demonstrates the library's whole thesis for $290.
8 Provenance record on every item, rendered on the page Turns "accessible" into a fact a customer can hand to their own procurement, and is hard for a bigger marketplace to copy.
9 Five categories deferred outright SNOMED, font licences, alarm tones, patient education, anything computing a recommendation. Each is a legal or regulatory exposure disproportionate to its revenue.
10 Public catalogue pages on the docs site A storefront behind sign-up has no top of funnel; the docs site is where the traffic and the SEO already are.

# Question My lean
1 Is this a revenue line or a delivery mechanism for the services motion? Delivery mechanism, with self-serve as lead qualification. It changes what "success" means in three months.
