Signature

The reference is a good signature pad. What healthcare needs is not a signature pad — it is a record of who committed to what, when, in what capacity, and by what means, including all the times nobody signed at all. This brief takes the supplied design as the starting point and works out the rest.

01 — The argument

A signature is not an image. It is a claim about a person.

Almost every signature component on npm solves one problem: get ink from a pointer onto a canvas and hand back a PNG. That problem is solved, it is solved well, and it is about fifteen percent of what a healthcare product needs.

The other eighty-five percent is everything the PNG does not say. Who signed — and were they the patient, the patient's daughter holding power of attorney, or the nurse who witnessed a patient too weak to hold a stylus? What were they agreeing to? Did they read it? What were they claiming — authorship, review, consent, or merely that they watched someone else sign? When, by whose clock? Against which version of the document? And, most often of all in real clinical work: what do you record when there is no signature?

A dash in a cell and a value that was never measured are different clinical facts — that argument is already the foundation of this library, and there is a lint rule enforcing it. The signature component is the same argument at a higher stake. An empty signature field and a patient who refused to sign are not the same fact. One is missing data. The other is a documented act with legal consequences, and a component whose only two states are empty and signed makes it unrecordable.

The thesis, in one line

The value of this component is a discriminated union of outcomes, not a base64 string. signed is one member. declined, unable, verbal, on-paper, pending and revoked are the others, and every one of them happens daily in a real clinic.

That reframing is what makes this an Oxygen component rather than a wrapper around signature_pad. It also happens to be the thing that sells: the buyers named in the readiness audit — Netsmart, CVS Health, Heidi Health — do not have a problem drawing ink. They have a problem passing an audit.

Three claims this brief will defend

Claim 1

Type is not a fallback

WCAG 2.1.1 — Level A — uses handwriting as its worked example of an input technique whose underlying function does not need a path. The Type tab is therefore a conformance mechanism, not a convenience. The reference already has it; it should stop looking like the second-best option.

Claim 2

Store strokes, render pixels

Capture an ordered stroke model, not a canvas. Undo becomes exact, export becomes lossless vector at any DPI, print stops being a 96 dpi smear, and forensic comparison becomes possible at all.

Claim 3

The manifest is the product

The read-only block that prints name, credential, capacity, meaning and timestamp beside the ink is what regulators actually look at. Nobody ships it. It is a day of work and it is the differentiator.

02 — The reference

What the supplied design gets right, and the eleven gaps

Taking the two screenshots at face value and reading them as a specification. The structure is sound and worth keeping: a modal, three capture methods as tabs, a bounded toolbar, one primary commit action that stays disabled until there is something to commit.

  • 1Keep: the modal shape. Title, close affordance, one primary commit. This is exactly antd's Modal with a custom footer, and it is the right container — signing is a deliberate, interruptible, confirmable act.
  • 2Gap: no attestation. Nothing above the pad says what signing means. A signature with no statement attached is, in the FDA's framing, a signature with no meaning — and in plain terms, the person does not know what they just agreed to.
  • 3Gap: no redo. "Back" is undo. Undo without redo is a trap: one over-tap destroys a good signature and the only path back is Clear and start again.
  • 4Gap: "Save Sign" has no destination. The label implies a saved-signature store, which raises the question the screenshot cannot answer: saved where, for how long, on a shared bedside tablet? This is a privacy decision wearing the costume of a toolbar button.
  • 5Gap: no signer identity. The ink is anonymous. Nothing captures printed name, credential, role, or capacity — and "capacity" is where healthcare diverges hardest from generic e-signature.
  • 6Gap: the only exit is X. A patient who declines, or who physically cannot sign, has no path. They are recorded identically to a form nobody opened.

03 — The outcome model

Seven things that happen when you ask someone to sign

This is the part of the design that is genuinely different, so it comes before the features rather than after them. Everything else in this brief is downstream of getting this right.

Ask a ward clerk what happens when they put a consent form in front of a patient and you get seven answers, not two. The component's value type is a discriminated union over exactly those seven, because a union is the only shape that makes the unhappy paths as expressible — and as type-checked — as the happy one.

Outcome What actually happened What the record must carry ISO / FHIR signature type
signed They signed. Draw, type or upload. Ink, method, signer, capacity, meaning, timestamp, document hash. Consent / Author's / Verification, per meaning
declined They read it and refused. A decision, not a failure. Reason (free text and/or coded), who recorded it, witness, timestamp. None — this is a Provenance entry, not a signature
unable They would sign but physically or cognitively cannot — intubated, restrained, casted, delirious, no assistive device to hand. Reason from a controlled list, mandatory witness, timestamp. Not the same as declined and must never be collapsed into it. Witness signature on the attestation
verbal Consent given by phone or video. Routine in telehealth. Mandatory witness, the words used, timestamp, channel. Event Witness / Verification
on-paper Wet signature on a form that will be scanned later, or already has been. Pointer to the scanned document, who witnessed the paper signing, timestamp. Source signature, target is the scan
pending Signed by one party, awaiting another — the classic resident note awaiting attending co-sign. Who signed so far, who is awaited, in what capacity, since when. Author's signature present; Coauthor's absent
revoked Consent withdrawn. The signature was valid and no longer is. The original signature, still rendered, plus who revoked it, when, and why. Never deleted. Original type, superseded
The one that will get argued about

declined and unable look like the same "didn't sign" bucket and are not. Declined is an exercise of autonomy and may need escalation, a second conversation, or a documented refusal of treatment. Unable is a capacity or physical barrier and usually triggers a witness or proxy pathway instead. Collapsing them loses the distinction that decides what happens next, which is exactly the failure mode AbsentValue exists to prevent one tier down.

Why a union rather than flags

A flags-and-nullable-fields shape ({ image?, declined?, reason?, witness? }) permits states that cannot exist — declined and an image, unable with no witness — and pushes the validation into every consumer. The union makes each outcome carry exactly its own evidence, and makes "unable without a witness" a compile error rather than a code review comment. 

// The whole component, in one type.

  | { outcome: "signed";    method: SignatureMethod; ink: Ink; signer: Signer;
      capacity: Capacity; meaning: SignatureMeaning; capturedAt: string;
      onBehalfOf?: Subject; documentHash?: string; provenance: CaptureContext }
  | { outcome: "declined";  reason: string; reasonCode?: string;
      recordedBy: Signer; witness?: Signer; recordedAt: string }
  | { outcome: "unable";    reason: UnableReason; detail?: string;
      witness: Signer;  // mandatory — not optional, by design
      recordedBy: Signer; recordedAt: string }
  | { outcome: "verbal";    script: string; channel: "phone" | "video" | "in-person";
      witness: Signer; recordedBy: Signer; recordedAt: string }
  | { outcome: "on-paper";  scanRef?: string; recordedBy: Signer; recordedAt: string }
  | { outcome: "pending";   awaiting: Capacity; since: string; sofar: SignatureValue[] }

06 — Accessibility

A draw-only signature pad fails WCAG at Level A

Not Level AA, and not on a technicality. This section sets out the argument carefully, including the counterargument, because it is the single most consequential design constraint on the component and it will be challenged.

6.1   The argument, at the right level

The tempting version of this argument is: drawing is a dragging movement, WCAG 2.2 added SC 2.5.7 requiring a single-pointer alternative, therefore a draw-only pad fails AA. That version is weaker than it looks, and it is worth knowing why before relying on it.

WCAG's normative glossary defines a dragging movement as one where an element follows the pointer between the down- and up-events. Freehand ink moves no element. An auditor can argue 2.5.7 does not apply. Worse, the W3C has already conceded the exception in the Understanding document for SC 2.5.1:

The sentence a vendor will quote at you

"An exception is made for functionality that is inherently and necessarily based on complex paths or multipoint gestures. For example, entering your signature may be inherently path-based (although acknowledging something or confirming your identity need not be)."

— Understanding SC 2.5.1 Pointer Gestures

Read only the bolded half and the essential exception appears to swallow the whole component. The parenthesis is what saves it, and it is the hinge of the entire argument: acknowledging something or confirming your identity need not be path-based. WCAG defines functionality as processes and outcomes achievable through user action. The function here is recording assent — not producing a bitmap of cursive. The technique is path-based; the function is not.

And that distinction is not an interpretation. It is stated in the Recommendation itself, in the normative note to a Level A criterion, using handwriting as the worked example:

The citation to lead with

"Note 1: This exception relates to the underlying function, not the input technique. For example, if using handwriting to enter text, the input technique (handwriting) requires path-dependent input but the underlying function (text input) does not."

— SC 2.1.1 Keyboard, Level A. Normative text, not guidance.

So the finding to put in the conformance report is a Level A keyboard failure, which is materially more serious than an AA dragging failure — and it is discharged by exactly the same mechanism the reference design already contains.

6.2   The two different text alternatives

SC 1.1.1 treats these as distinct cases and getting them the same way round is the common bug:

  • The capture surface accepts input, so it falls under the Controls, Input exception and needs a name that describes its purpose — not a description. "Draw your signature here". A long description here is wrong.
  • The completed signature is non-text content presented to the user, and needs an alternative serving the equivalent purpose. The equivalent purpose of a signature image is whose it is and that it was given — never the shape of the strokes. So: alt="Signature of Josh Randall, signed 16 August 2026", and never alt="handwritten signature".

6.3   There is no ARIA role for a drawing surface

<canvas> has no implicit role and permits any role, which is total freedom and zero guidance. There is no role meaning "freehand drawing surface", and that absence is itself an argument for the architecture:

  1. Do not make the canvas the control. It is the visual affordance for one mode. The operable control tree is native HTML.
  2. Wrap the widget in role="group" with aria-labelledby at the visible label and aria-describedby at the instructions. That is what satisfies 1.3.1.
  3. Expose Draw / Type / Upload as a real tablist, so the accessible path is discoverable rather than merely present.
  4. Announce state through a polite live region — "Signature captured", "Signature cleared". Canvas bitmap changes are invisible to assistive technology and nothing else will report them.
  5. role="img" is correct for the finished signature and wrong for the surface. On the surface it asserts a non-interactive graphic, which is a lie about an element that takes input, and it discards state — a direct 4.1.2 problem.

6.4   Forced colors will erase the signature

Forced-colors mode rewrites CSS <color> properties. A canvas bitmap is painted by script, so the ink pixels are never recoloured — while the container's background is. The classic result is black ink on a transparent canvas inside a forced-black container: the signature simply disappears. Three fixes, all cheap:

  • Paint an opaque background into the bitmap before inking, so ink and ground always travel together — and the exported PNG becomes self-contained as a side effect.
  • Re-theme under @media (forced-colors: active), reading CanvasText / Canvas from a probe element and repainting the stroke. Repaint on media-query change.
  • Give the field a real CSS border, not a shadow or a gradient — box-shadow computes to none under forced colors and a faked boundary vanishes with it.

The typed path needs none of this. It is plain DOM text and is system-coloured for free — an argument for the same design decision that has nothing to do with WCAG conformance and everything to do with the component still working.

6.5   The population, not the edge case

Worth stating plainly because it changes how the trade-off feels: the people asked to sign in a healthcare product are disproportionately those with tremor, Parkinson's, arthritis, post-stroke weakness, neuropathy, or a cannula in the back of the signing hand. Stroke smoothing helps a little. The typed path is the answer, and treating it as the accommodation rather than the norm gets the design backwards.

One honest caveat

Two links in this chain are inference rather than quotation. SC 3.3.4's Understanding document never names healthcare consent — "a contract of any type" is the bridge, and it is a strong bridge but it is a bridge. And no primary source states that canvas bitmaps are exempt from forced colors; it follows from the spec enumerating only CSS colour properties, and it matches observed behaviour. Both are flagged here rather than presented as settled.

07 — API

Four components and a hook, shaped like Ant Design

antd names things plainly — Upload, Rate, Transfer, Mentions — and composes with dot notation: Input.TextArea, Upload.Dragger, Card.Meta. This follows both conventions.

The form control

<Signature />

What you put in a Form.Item. Renders the empty affordance or the captured result, opens the modal, and speaks the value/onChange contract. This is the default export and the only thing most people touch.

The capture surface

<Signature.Pad />

Just the pad: canvas, toolbar, stroke model. No modal, no tabs, no identity fields. For products that want the capture inline, or want to build their own chrome around it.

The dialog

<Signature.Modal />

The screen in §5.1 — tabs, identity, attestation, outcomes. Usable standalone when the trigger is somewhere else entirely, such as a toolbar action on a document viewer.

The record

<Signature.Manifest />

Read-only. Takes a SignatureValue and renders §5.5, including the non-signed outcomes. Prints correctly. The piece that justifies the whole component.

The common case

Three antd contracts this satisfies exactly

1. The custom-control contract is three props — id, value, onChange. The id is what Form.Item passes so <label htmlFor> resolves; a component that ignores it has a decorative label. 2. The value is passed directly, not wrapped in an event, matching Select and DatePicker rather than Input — so no getValueFromEvent is needed. 3. Validation state is read from context with Form.Item.useStatus(), which is how antd's own controls get their error border; an explicit status prop is accepted too, for use outside a form.

08 — Data contract

FHIR, and the thing everyone gets wrong

The library already ships @oxygenui-design/fhir, so the signature component should emit FHIR rather than a bespoke shape. One finding here is load-bearing and surprising.

The finding

The Consent resource has no signature element. Not in R4, not in R5. Neither does Composition.attester nor DocumentReference.attester. Only Provenance.signature, Contract.signer.signature and Bundle.signature carry the Signature datatype at all.

So a component that "produces a consent signature" cannot emit one resource. It must emit a pair: the consent or attestation resource, plus a Provenance whose .target points at it and whose .signature carries the ink. The output type is a transaction Bundle, and designing it as anything else means rework later.

The Signature datatype

ElementTypeR4R5What we put in it
typeCoding1..*0..*The ISO E1762 code for the meaning prop.
wheninstant1..10..1The injected server time. Not Consent.date — see below.
whoReference(…)1..10..1The signer. Practitioner, Patient, RelatedPerson, Device, Organization.
onBehalfOfReference(…)0..10..1The proxy case. Set whenever capacity ≠ "self".
targetFormatcode0..10..1Which bytes were signed — application/fhir+json.
sigFormatcode0..10..1image/png for the ink.
database64Binary0..10..1The trimmed PNG.

The only R4→R5 change is cardinality relaxation on the first three. That makes the rule simple: always write the R4 shape. R4's stricter cardinality is a strict subset of what R5 accepts, so populating type, when and who unconditionally produces a payload valid in both releases. Do not let R5's relaxation tempt you into omitting who — it is the element that makes a signature attributable at all.

The codes are OIDs, not integers

A detail worth stating because it is easy to get wrong from the prose pages: the codes in urn:iso-astm:E1762-95:2013 are full dotted OID strings, not the short numbers the display order suggests. Five of the eighteen matter here.

CodeDisplayWhen this component emits it

1.2.840.10065.1.12.1.1Author's Signaturemeaning="author" — a clinician signing their own note.
1.2.840.10065.1.12.1.5Verification Signaturemeaning="verification" — countersigning a verbal order.
1.2.840.10065.1.12.1.8Signature WitnessThe witness on unable, verbal, or an uploaded image.

Note that .1.11 Consent Witness means witnessing the counselling, and .1.8 Signature Witness means witnessing the signing. They are different events and the component should not conflate them. R5 additionally admits ETSI codes (ProofOfApproval and friends) that R4 does not; since the binding is only preferred this does not break interop, but emitting them would look foreign to an R4 consumer, so we do not.

Where each signature lives

The signatureHome
Patient consentConsent + Provenance.signature with type = …1.7, who = Patient, Provenance.target → Consent. Human-readable form on Consent.sourceAttachment.
Clinician attestationComposition.attester with mode = legal or professional for the human-readable fact, plus Provenance.signature for the bytes. attester alone records that someone attested but cannot carry the ink.

Two release traps to abstract behind an adapter

Consent.scope was removed in R5, and Consent.dateTime became Consent.date — renamed and narrowed from dateTime to date, which silently discards the time of day. Never use Consent.date as the signing timestamp; that is what Signature.when is for, and it is an instant. Also renamed: patient → subject, and provision.type moved up to a root-level Consent.decision.

Say what a PNG is not

sigFormat: "image/png" is an honest declaration that this is a graphical signature — a picture of a mark, carrying no cryptographic integrity guarantee whatsoever. Anyone can crop it out and paste it somewhere else. Integrators routinely assume Signature implies cryptographic signing, and a component that does not say otherwise is letting them.

Where non-repudiation is actually required, the mature pattern is two entries in the same Provenance.signature array sharing who and when: one image/png holding the ink for human review, one application/jose holding a JWS over the canonical resource bytes with targetFormat: "application/fhir+json". The component emits the first and leaves a documented seam for the second, because signing keys are a deployment concern and do not belong in a UI component.

09 — Compliance

What the law actually requires, and what it does not

A component library cannot make a customer compliant. It can make compliance reachable — by capturing what the regulations require and refusing to imply guarantees it does not provide. Every quotation below is from the primary source and linked.

9.1   21 CFR Part 11 — and why it shapes the manifest

Part 11 governs electronic records and signatures in FDA-regulated contexts — clinical trials, device and pharmaceutical manufacturing. It does not govern ordinary clinical care records. But §11.50 is worth designing to regardless, because it is a good specification for a signature manifestation and it costs nothing to satisfy:

21 CFR §11.50(a) — Signature manifestations

Signed electronic records must contain information showing the printed name of the signer, the date and time when the signature was executed, and the meaning — "such as review, approval, responsibility, or authorship" — associated with the signature.

§11.50(b) adds that these must be subject to the same controls as the record itself and included in any human-readable form, displayed or printed.

— 21 CFR 11.50

That is the manifest in §5.5, almost line for line, and it is why meaning is a required prop rather than a nicety: a signature that does not say what it meant fails the only clause in Part 11 that describes what a signature should look like on screen.

Where the component stops

§11.200(a) requires non-biometric electronic signatures to employ at least two distinct identification components, with all components used at the first signing in a continuous session and at least one component "only executable by, and designed to be used only by, the individual" for subsequent signings — and requires that unauthorized use "requires collaboration of two or more individuals."

That is authentication, and it is not a UI component's job. A signature pad does not know who is holding the stylus. The component renders the identity the host asserts and records the ink; the identity assurance behind it belongs to the application's session and auth layer. Saying so plainly in the API documentation is the difference between a customer building a compliant system and a customer assuming they already have one.

— 21 CFR 11.200

9.2   ESIGN — the typed name is a real signature

This is the legal fact the accessibility argument in §06 leans on, and it is worth quoting exactly because the design decision rests on it. The ESIGN Act defines an electronic signature as:

15 U.S.C. §7006(5)

"an electronic sound, symbol, or process, attached to or logically associated with a contract or other record and executed or adopted by a person with the intent to sign the record."

— 15 U.S.C. 7006

Nothing in that definition mentions handwriting, cursive, or a path. A typed name is a symbol, adopted, with intent — and the legal weight sits entirely on the intent, which is what the attestation statement and the "Sign and continue" button exist to establish. The drawn mark is one way to manifest intent; it is not the thing that creates the obligation.

This closes the loop on §06. WCAG's "essential" exception fails when the outcome can be achieved another way that conforms. It can be, and the statute is what proves it. So the copy in mockup 5.2 — "Typing your name here has the same legal effect as signing by hand" — is accurate, and removing the user's hesitation about it is a conformance act as much as a usability one.

Three further authorities make the point harder than a definitional argument alone would:

  • UETA's Official Comments say it outright. Comment 7 to §2: "No specific technology need be used in order to create a valid signature." And Comment 1 to §9 lists worked examples of attributable signatures, of which the first is simply: "The person types his/her name as part of an e-mail purchase order."
  • The IRS lists them as equals. Publication 1345 (Rev. 12-2025) enumerates acceptable electronic signature methods under the heading "No specific technology is required", and the same list contains both "a handwritten signature, mark or command input on a display screen by a stylus device" and "a typed name … typed into a signature block on a website form by a signer". A federal agency, in a current publication, treating the drawn and typed paths as co-equal members of one list is about as direct as this gets.
  • No state may lawfully prefer the drawn one. ESIGN's preemption clause, 15 U.S.C. §7002(a)(2)(A)(ii), permits state variation only where it does "not require, or accord greater legal status or effect to, the implementation or application of a specific technology". A rule demanding a drawn mark and refusing a typed name is exactly that.
The counter-case — and it is a design requirement, not a caveat

Cunningham v. Zurich American Insurance Co. (Tex. App. 2011) held that an email signature block was not a signature, because "nothing in the email suggests" it was typed "with the intention that the block be her signature" — and no evidence showed it "was typed purposefully rather than generated automatically." Later courts disagreed with it, but the reasoning matters more than the outcome: it failed on evidence of intent, not on typed-name-ness.

Every authority in this section turns on the same single element — intent to sign. So the typed path must be built to evidence it:

  • Never pre-fill the typed signature field. An autofilled name is precisely the Cunningham fact pattern. The identity field in 5.1 may be pre-filled; the signature field in 5.2 must be typed by the person signing.
  • Label the field as the signature, not as a name — the label is evidence.
  • Carry the attestation and an explicit affirmation into the value, so the record shows what the intent attached to.
  • Record the timestamp and the keystroke-origin method in the capture context.

9.3   Medical-record authentication

42 CFR §482.24(c)(1) — CMS Conditions of Participation

"All patient medical record entries must be legible, complete, dated, timed, and authenticated in written or electronic form by the person responsible for providing or evaluating the service provided, consistent with hospital policies and procedures."

— 42 CFR 482.24

Two design consequences. "Dated, timed" is why the timestamp is injected from the server rather than read from the browser clock. And "by the person responsible for providing or evaluating the service" is why capacity is a first-class field — the rule is about who, and a component that captures only a mark cannot evidence it.

9.4   Biometric privacy — the good news

The obvious worry about capturing stroke dynamics is Illinois BIPA, which carries a private right of action and has produced the largest biometric settlements in the US. The statute answers the question directly:

740 ILCS 14/10 — Illinois Biometric Information Privacy Act

"'Biometric identifier' means a retina or iris scan, fingerprint, voiceprint, or scan of hand or face geometry. Biometric identifiers do not include writing samples, written signatures, photographs, human biological samples used for valid scientific testing or screening, demographic data, tattoo descriptions, or physical descriptions…"

And, separately: "Biometric identifiers do not include information captured from a patient in a health care setting or information collected, used, or stored for health care treatment, payment, or operations under" HIPAA.

Texas reaches the same result by a different route: CUBI's definition is a closed enumeration — "a retina or iris scan, fingerprint, voiceprint, or record of hand or face geometry" — with no signature in it and no exclusion list at all. Enforcement there is the Attorney General's alone, at up to $25,000 per violation, with no private right of action. (Texas amended §503.001 effective 1 January 2026 via HB 149; the definition was renumbered to (a)(2) but its wording is unchanged.)

Which does not make it a free action

A written signature is excluded. Whether stroke dynamics — the timing, velocity and pressure series behind it — are a "writing sample" or something else is not settled by the text, and the argument that they are a behavioural biometric is not frivolous. The design position stands: capture them into the model because the model needs them to render, never emit them by default, and make captureBiometrics an explicit decision the integrator makes with their counsel rather than a default they inherit from us.

Why core is separate

The engine is testable without a DOM

Synthetic pointer sequences in, deterministic Stroke[] out, golden SVG compared as text. No canvas pixel-diffing, no headless browser, no flake. It is also what makes a future custom-element build — or a React Native one — a shell rather than a rewrite.

Why one geometry

SVG and canvas from the same path

The loaders in this library already carry a parity test proving the React and custom-element renderings agree; it caught a mistyped bezier and a missing keyframe. The same technique applies: render the same strokes to both targets and assert the path data matches.

The thing not to build

Do not reimplement variable-width Bézier stroke smoothing. signature_pad has solved it, and the three most-used React signature packages are all wrappers around it. Reimplementing that maths is a tarpit with no differentiation at the end of it. Wrap it behind signature-core's interface and spend the effort on everything above the ink — the outcome model, the manifest, the antd surface, the FHIR mapping. That is where the value is and where nobody else is working.

@zag-js/signature-pad is worth reading before writing a line: it is a headless state machine that already models the hidden form input, the label association, and the guide line. It is the closest prior art to the accessible architecture in §6.3.

11 — The Ant Design question

Two decisions to make before any code is written

"We will follow Ant Design" is clear as a direction and ambiguous as an instruction. There are two separate questions inside it and they have different answers.

Time-sensitive, and new since the instruction was given

Ant Design v6.6.0 shipped on 10 August 2026 — six days ago. The v5 line ended at 5.29.3, and ant.design/components/* now serves v6 documentation; v5's docs have moved to 5x.ant.design. A component designed this week against v5 is being designed against a superseded major, and will meet v6 applications.

11.1   Which major

The renames that touch this component directly. None is deep — they are almost all prop-name churn — but they decide what the API table in §07 actually says.

11.2   Wrap, match, or migrate

The bigger question, and the one that decides what this library is. Today it is a shadcn-style registry: components are copied into the customer's repo as readable source, styled with Tailwind v4 utilities generated from DTCG tokens, with no antd dependency anywhere. Ant Design is a different architecture, not a different skin.

Option A

Wrap antd

Compose real antd components; antd becomes a peer dependency.

Gets: Modal's focus trap, Tabs' full ARIA and roving tabindex, Form wiring, theming — free and correct.

Costs: ends copy-as-source distribution, makes every consumer carry antd, and couples the roadmap to antd's majors. The registry channel effectively closes.

Option B

Match antd

Independent implementations that follow antd's API and visual language, with Oxygen's token layer mapped onto antd's token names.

Gets: keeps the registry, keeps "readable source you own", keeps the token pipeline and its contrast gate.

Costs: reimplements Modal and Tabs behaviour, including the accessibility antd already got right. Real work, easy to get subtly wrong.

Option C

Migrate wholesale

Adopt antd as the foundation and retire the registry channel.

Gets: one system, no impedance, fastest per-component velocity from here.

Costs: discards the token pipeline, the brand axis, the dual-channel distribution, and the differentiator in the strategy — that customers own the source.

Recommendation, with the reasoning visible

Option A for this component specifically; defer the general decision. The signature component is unusually antd-shaped — it is a Modal containing Tabs and a Form control, which is three of the four things antd does best and where its accessibility is genuinely good (Tabs ship correct role="tablist", roving tabindex, arrow-key navigation and aria-controls wiring). Reimplementing a focus-trapped modal to avoid a peer dependency, for a component whose entire argument is accessibility, is the wrong trade.

Meanwhile signature-core has no antd dependency at all, so the expensive half of the work is portable whichever way the general decision lands. That is the hedge: build the engine framework-free, wrap antd for the surface, and let one component's experience inform the library-wide call rather than deciding it in the abstract.

12 — Test plan

What CI has to hold, and how to test a canvas without flake

The library already runs 852 tests, a story-derived accessibility sweep, deterministic visual regression, and six cross-framework smoke apps in three engines. This slots into that machinery rather than inventing a second one.

The governing idea

Never pixel-diff a canvas in a unit test. Feed synthetic pointer sequences into signature-core, get a deterministic Stroke[] out, and compare the rendered SVG as text. Same input, byte-identical output, no headless browser, no flake. Pixels are checked exactly once, in the Playwright visual-regression layer, where the existing config already disables animation and fixes the device scale factor.

What only you can decide

Six of these are cheap now and expensive later. They are ordered by how much of the code they change.

1
Wrap antd, match antd, or migrate to antd?

2

Do saved signatures exist?
The reference's "Save Sign" implies a store, and a store implies a location, a retention period, and a policy for shared bedside devices. My recommendation: not in v1. Ship "saved with this form only", which is a defensible sentence, and add reuse when there is a named customer and a storage decision behind it.
4
Are stroke biometrics ever captured?
Timing, velocity and pressure have genuine forensic value and genuine privacy exposure. My recommendation: capture into the model, never emit by default, behind an explicit captureBiometrics flag with the legal position documented at the call site. See §09 for what the biometric-privacy statutes actually say about handwritten signatures.
5
Is 21 CFR Part 11 in scope for the first release?
It applies to FDA-regulated records — clinical trials, device and pharma manufacturing — and not to ordinary clinical care. If a target customer is in that space the manifest and the audit-event stream need to be designed to its requirements from P1 rather than retro-fitted. My recommendation: design the manifest to satisfy it regardless, because it costs almost nothing and it is the difference between passing and failing a procurement review.
6
Which comes first — this, or the Phase 4 catalog?
The readiness audit's Phase 4 queued StatusBadge, AbsentValue, Alert, Skeleton and EmptyState, and 86 component-tier tokens are currently orphaned waiting for them. This component is four weeks and consumes almost none of those tokens. Both are defensible; they are not the same bet. Catalog breadth makes the library adoptable. The signature component makes it differentiated, and nobody else in the antd ecosystem has one.

Naming

antd names plainly and composes with dots, so Signature with .Pad, .Modal and .Manifest is the native-feeling choice. The alternatives considered and rejected:

CandidateVerdict
SignatureRecommended. antd's own instinct — Upload, Rate, Transfer. Short, obvious, and the dot-composition reads naturally.
SignaturePadWhat every existing library calls itself, which is precisely the problem: it names the fifteen percent of the component that is not the point.
ESignatureLegalistic, dated, and invites the assumption of cryptographic signing that §08 works hard to deny.
AttestationRight for the clinician case, wrong for patient consent. Too narrow.
SignatureFieldAccurate but redundant once it lives in Form.Item.

npm package: @oxygenui-design/signature and @oxygenui-design/signature-core, matching the existing scope and the loaders' split.

Where this leaves us

The reference design is a sound starting point and about a third of the finished component. The two-thirds that is missing — the outcome model, the identity and capacity capture, the manifest, and the accessible path treated as the primary path rather than the fallback — is also the two-thirds that nobody else has built, in a category where Ant Design itself ships nothing at all.

Answer the six questions above and P1 can start the same day.
