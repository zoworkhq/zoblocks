Home page · Data Grid section

Five ways to show the caseload.

Same six clients, same six columns, same behavioural health data. What changes is the furniture around it and how much room the numbers get.

No status bar, no footnote wall

The shipped section wraps eight rows in six bands of chrome. Nothing below has more than two, and three have none.

Rules only where they do work

A hairline under every column turns a table into a grid of boxes. Only 01 keeps vertical rules, and it keeps them on purpose.

Room to read

Rows run 44–72px instead of 27, and the score is set large enough to scan a column without reading it.

Variation 01

Open ledger

The current design with the chrome removed and the air put back. One coverage line above, one footnote line below, and nothing else — no status bar, no identity strip, no provenance band. Rows at 68px, the PHQ-9 set at 18px so a column of scores reads as a shape.

Keeps
Every claim the component makes, in the least furniture that can carry them.
Loses
The live beat. Held results become a quiet pill rather than a band.
Risk
Closest to the thing you already said looks messy — it is the same idea, disciplined.

Variation 02

Card rows

Each client is a card with space between them, so the row is the unit rather than the cell. No rules at all — separation is done with gaps and a border. Risk becomes a meter, the program a pill, and the whole thing lifts on hover like something you can act on.

Keeps
Air, and an obvious affordance: every row reads as clickable.
Loses
Scanability down a column. Gaps break the vertical rhythm numbers rely on.
Risk
Reads as a generic SaaS list. It is the least clinical of the five.

Variation 03

Split panel

Identity on the left in the page's own ground, measures on the right in a tinted one. Two zones instead of six columns of equal weight, which is how a clinician actually reads the row: who, then what. Each measure gets a label above it, so no header row is needed at all.

Keeps
Identity legible at a glance, and the values grouped as a set rather than a queue.
Loses
Column alignment across rows — labels repeat on every row.
Risk
Repeating the labels six times is a lot of small type. Good at six rows, poor at sixty.

Variation 04

Editorial

No panel, no border, no background — the table sits directly on the page like a figure in a printed report. Coverage is a sentence in body copy above it. The PHQ-9 is set at 28px in the display face, because on a home page the number is the thing you want somebody to look at first.

Keeps
The most air of the five, and the largest numbers. Confident.
Loses
The sense that this is a component. It reads as page content, not as UI.
Risk
A visitor may not register that they are looking at something installable.

Variation 05

Focus rail

A tighter table beside a rail that shows whichever row is selected in full. Click a row and the rail follows. The footnotes go with it — provenance and the Part 2 notice belong to the row a reader is on, not to the bottom of the page — which is what lets the table itself carry no chrome whatsoever.

Keeps
Density and explanation at once, and the identity control the component argues for.
Loses
19rem of width, and it needs interaction to make its point.
Risk
Two things to look at. On a scroll-past home page that can be one too many.

If you want my pick

04 Editorial for the frame, 01's table inside it. The panel is what was making it look busy — six nested bands of furniture around eight rows. Dropping the panel entirely gives the numbers the most air of any option and puts the coverage claim in body copy where it reads as an argument rather than as a UI label; 01's ruled table keeps a column of scores scannable in a way 02's gaps do not.

05 is the one to build second, on the component page rather than the home page. The rail is where footnotes actually belong, and it is the only variation that shows the identity line doing its job without a visitor having to know what to look for.
