01
Where this lands

Executive summary

Oxygen UI is a better product than its website admits, and the website is costing you the only conversion that matters. The library is the strongest healthcare component work I can find in the open — and the site presents it as a component library, to developers, with no path from “this is impressive” to “we should talk to the team that built this.”

Four things are true at once, and the redesign has to hold all four.

The asset

The substance is real and it is rare

26 components typed to FHIR R4, 3,458 tests, an 18-rule lint plugin that makes unsafe renders unwritable, a contrast gate across 23 token pairs, six frameworks driven in three engines. Almost nobody in healthcare has this. It is barely visible on the site.

The gap

There is no buyer on the page

Navigation reads Components · Showcase · Marketplace · Pro, then Sign in / Sign up. There is no services surface, no engagement offer, no proof of who built it, and no route for an executive who will never read a prop table.

The category

The competitive field is abandoned, not crowded

Cerner’s Terra UI was archived in June 2024. Nord is an internal system published as a courtesy, with no commercial motion. Medplum documents its React library in Storybook only. Behavioral health has no component library at all. This is occupation, not competition.

The risk

The proof is the part that is broken

The homepage’s single most persuasive section — the side-by-side of a hand-rolled renderer against Oxygen — is currently an empty grid reading catalog rebuilding. The three headline statistics render as 0 in the server HTML. Both are verifiable today.

What I recommend

Keep the current design and finish the site around it. I did not start there — I built three separate sets of five alternative directions, and none of them beat what ships. That turned out to be the useful result: the visual system is the most finished part of this product, and every serious problem I found is content, completeness or conversion.

§11 is the plan: eleven prioritised changes, each drawn in the site's own tokens and type so you can see it as the current design with work done to it. Three of them are defects worth fixing this week. Five are the difference between a library site and a business site. Three are platform work that makes the next hundred components cheap.

The three that carry most of the value:

  1. Put a buyer on the page. An engagement band with four named services, a named engineer, and an offer specific enough to qualify. There is currently no team, no services and no contact anywhere on the site — a visitor who has decided to hire you cannot find out how.
  2. Finish the comparison and halve the homepage. The most persuasive block is a 183 px empty grid; the catalogue below it is 5,345 px of a 10,359 px page. Both are shown fixed in §12.
  3. Render the argument on the server. Every number and every state present in the initial HTML — because two of your highest-value readers are an AI answer engine and a procurement researcher, and neither runs your IntersectionObserver.
The one sentence The site’s job is not to explain components. It is to make a healthcare CTO think whoever wrote these invariants has shipped clinical software before — and then give them somewhere to click.
02
What the site is for

Business objective

The library is lead generation for a services and enterprise-contract business. Every design decision in this report is made against that, and where a decision would serve component adoption at the expense of client conversion, I have said so.

The model, stated plainly

The recorded strategy is that revenue comes from services and team/enterprise contracts in the $2.5k–50k range; the free core and any cheap individual tier are lead generation rather than a revenue line. The buyer is digital-health startups, health-tech vendors and their agencies — not provider groups or payers, who buy finished products rather than component libraries.

That has a sharp consequence the current site does not reflect: a component install is not the conversion. It is the top of a funnel whose bottom is a conversation. A site optimised purely for installs is optimising a vanity metric.

The layering problem, and why it is the whole design brief

These audiences want contradictory things from the same pixels. The executive wants less information; the engineer wants more. The usual resolutions are both bad: a marketing site with a docs subdomain splits the proof from the pitch, and a single averaged page serves nobody.

The resolution I am proposing is progressive disclosure of construction rather than of content. Every visitor sees the same working clinical surface. What differs is how far in they choose to look — and the control that takes them further is visible, single, and on every page. The executive never has to opt out of detail; the engineer never has to hunt for it.

Consequence for content Nothing on the site may be written for one audience in a way that repels another. A prop table is fine — it is below a plain sentence about what the component refuses to do. That ordering is the entire trick, and §7 makes it a rule.
04
What is there now

Current experience critique

I ran the site locally at localhost:6001 and read the source. This section is what I found, not what I assume. Three of these are defects you can reproduce in a minute; the rest are strategic.

Method pnpm --filter @oxygenui-design/docs dev against commit ba4543c. Note that the docs app aliases seven workspace packages to their dist/ output, so a fresh checkout must build those packages before dev renders anything — worth a line in the README.

Defects, in severity order

D1 · the proof is a placeholder

“Same five results. Two renderers.” renders an empty grid

The section that makes the argument better than any paragraph on the site currently shows “The side-by-side comparison renders the real component — it returns with the rebuilt component catalog.” This is the single highest-value block on the homepage and it is a stub labelled CATALOG REBUILDING.

D2 · the headline numbers are zero

The server HTML says 0 states, 0 modes, 0 dependencies

Counter initialises at useState(0) and only counts up when an IntersectionObserver fires. Every non-JS reader — Google’s first pass, an LLM crawler, a social preview, a printed page — reads three zeros where the argument should be. The fix is one line: render to, animate down from it.

D3 · the page below the fold is invisible

47 elements at opacity: 0 pending an observer

Everything past the hero carries data-reveal. It is a lovely effect when it runs. When it does not — a hidden tab, a reduced-motion edge case, a crawler that paints once — the page is blank. Reveal should decorate content that is already there, never gate it.

D4 · the homepage is 10,117 px of catalogue

26 component cards, inline, each with live art

It is genuinely impressive engineering and it is the wrong page. An executive scrolls past twenty-six technical cards to reach a closing CTA. The catalogue is a destination (/components) and already exists there; the homepage needs six of them and a workflow.

Strategic gaps

  • No buyer surface. Nav is Components · Showcase · Marketplace · Pro, then Sign in / Sign up. There is no team, no engagement offer, no contact, no case work. A visitor who has decided to hire you has nowhere to go.
  • Sign-up before value. /pro is “Create an organisation, or sign back in” — a login wall on a surface whose job is to persuade.
  • The showcase disclaims itself. “Reference implementations, not customers.” Admirably honest, and it leaves the credibility slot empty rather than filling it with the proof you do have — the engineering record, the decision log, the regulatory reading.
  • No clinical pattern layer. The components are excellent and atomised. There is nothing at the level of “how a critical result gets acknowledged”, which is the level at which a healthcare buyer thinks and searches.
  • Dark-first by default. Defensible for a developer tool. It is the wrong first impression for a clinical product, because clinical software is used in lit rooms and every screenshot a buyer recognises is light.

What is genuinely good, and must survive the redesign

Stated plainly, because the defect list above understates it The current visual system is accomplished. It is light-first with confident, tightly-set display type; the hero is a composed object — layered cards at different elevations forming a live instrument panel — rather than a headline beside a screenshot; and the typographic detail is real (axis labels at 0.625rem on 0.18em tracking, tabular numerals throughout, a two-tone focus ring engineered to survive both grounds). The .instrument bezel, its 28 px graph-paper face, the cursor-tracked glow and the single sweeping telemetry trace are a coherent hardware metaphor, executed with care. The problems in this section are problems of content, completeness and conversion. They are not evidence that the design needs replacing — and any replacement has to clear this bar, not merely differ from it.
  • The layered hero object. The right half of the fold is a designed illustration assembled from live components, with real depth. It is the single strongest visual asset on the site and the hardest thing to replace.
  • The four-theme token system. Light, dark, high-contrast at a 7:1 floor, and a brand axis — with the site chrome carrying its own palette per theme so the page never fights the components.
  • The writing. “Those aren’t edge cases. They are Tuesday.” That is a better sentence than anything on any competitor site. The voice is the brand; do not sand it off.
  • The component card art. Each card renders a real clinical state — an amended HbA1c, a signed-out hospitalist, a haemolysed specimen. That is a differentiator hiding in a grid.
  • The loaders. Paced to resting physiology, clamped below 1.7 Hz, with a designed still state. Nobody else has thought about this and it is a legitimate brand moment.
  • The component page skeleton. Eleven anchored sections including conformance and source. The bones of the documentation standard in §9 are already there.

Documentation craft — what to steal from outside healthcare

shadcn/ui

Distribution as the product

A registry schema, a CLI, GitHub integration and an MCP server, with source presented as the thing you own. Explicitly optimised for LLMs to read. Oxygen already has this architecture — it is under-claimed.

Stripe

The reader’s context persists

Language choice, keys and account context follow you across every page. The docs equivalent here is framework, density and terminology — set once, honoured everywhere.

Mantine

The props playground

The best interactive prop editor in the field. Its weakness is that controls are hand-declared per demo — which is precisely the drift Oxygen’s schema prevents. Match the interaction, generate the controls.

Radix / Base UI

Accessibility as spec

Keyboard maps and ARIA published as tables, per component. Oxygen can go further: publish the assertion, not the intention — the test name next to the claim.

Linear

Restraint as signal

Almost no marketing language. Density, precision and motion carry the quality claim. The closest existing analogue to the tone Oxygen’s writing already has.

NHS

Users search for “ask a user for their NHS number”, not for “text input”. The pattern layer is both the better information architecture and the better SEO.

Enterprise healthcare vendors — how the buyer is actually reached

Abridge, Innovaccer and Commure are not competitors, but they are the sites your buyer reads before yours. Two observations transfer directly.

You will not have a KLAS ranking. Your equivalent is publicly verifiable engineering evidence: the CI record, the decision log, the regulatory reading. That is a real substitute and it is already sitting in the repository unused.

The claim

Positioning strategy

The current line — “Healthcare UI that already knows what the data means” — is good. It is also a claim about a library. The positioning has to carry a claim about a team, because the team is what is for sale.

The positioning statement

Recommended

Oxygen UI is the interface layer for clinical software — the components, and the people who build them.

For teams shipping FHIR-facing products, it renders the states a record actually contains — preliminary, amended, restricted, uninterpreted, refused — and enforces the rules that keep those states honest. It exists because the team behind it builds clinical software, and it is the part of that practice that fits in a package.

The second sentence does the work the current site omits. It is the bridge from artefact to practice, and it is the only reason an executive stays.

Three pillars, and the evidence under each

Pillar 1

Clinically literate

It knows what the data means, not just its shape.

Evidence: seven signature outcomes including refusal · administrative gender is not a renderable field · a stated interpretation always beats a derived one · four kinds of absence, each named.

Pillar 2

Enforced, not documented

The invariants are gates. A dangerous render does not compile.

Evidence: 18 lint rules · 23 contrast pairs × themes · 3,458 tests · dependency-cruiser layer rules · gen --strict blocks a component without a story.

Pillar 3

Built by practitioners

This is a working method, published — not a side project.

Evidence: the ADR log · the regulatory reading in §10 · the engineering standard · four named engagement types.

What to stop saying

  • “Component library.” Accurate, and it files you next to 200 alternatives and under the wrong buying intent. Say interface layer for clinical software.
  • “Beautiful / modern / delightful.” Absent from the current site, to its credit. Keep it that way as marketing pressure grows.
  • Anything implying compliance. The existing “not a compliance boundary” disclaimer is exactly right and should be promoted from the footer to a real page. In this market, the vendor who explains what they are not is the one who gets believed about what they are.

Naming and the risk you have already accepted

The name collides with an existing WSO2 React design system, with oxygen-ui.vercel.app, and with Soflyy’s Oxygen trademark in the page-builder category. The recorded decision is to proceed, never brand as bare “Oxygen”, stay out of page-builder territory, and not contest the head term.

The site should carry that decision visually. “Oxygen UI” always appears as a two-word lockup with the mark, never as “Oxygen” alone in a heading, and the <title> of every page carries a healthcare qualifier so the SERP entry disambiguates itself before anyone clicks. This is free defence and costs one style rule.

07
How it is written

Content strategy

The existing writing is the best asset on the site. This section is mostly about extending it to three audiences it currently does not address, and one rule that makes the layering work.

The ordering rule

Rule On any page, in any block: the claim in plain language, then the mechanism, then the evidence. Never mechanism first. An executive stops after sentence one and has understood something true; an engineer reads all three and finds nothing skipped.

Applied to a real component:

1 · Claim — everyone

“A patient who refused to sign and a form nobody opened are different facts with different consequences.”

2 · Mechanism — technical

“The value is a discriminated union over seven outcomes, not string | null.”

3 · Evidence — evaluator

“unable without a witness is a compile error. Emits a FHIR transaction Bundle, because Consent carries no signature element in R4 or R5.”

That is three sentences already written in your README. The content work is largely relocating existing prose into this order, not producing new prose.

The demo-data standard

Generic placeholder data is the fastest way to lose a clinical reader, and the current site already avoids it. Make it a written rule so it survives contributors:

  • Names are plausible and diverse across the catalogue — Okonkwo, Marsh, Boateng, Adeyemi, Vance. Never John Doe.
  • Values are clinically coherent: a potassium of 6.8 arrives with a rate of change and an acknowledgement requirement, because that is what a real one carries.
  • Every demo set over-represents the hard states — this is already what @oxygenui-design/fixtures does. Say so on the page; it is a differentiator.
  • Systems are reserved example.org URIs and the synthetic-data statement is visible, not buried. It answers a procurement question before it is asked.

Terminology as a product feature

The recorded plan makes terminology a runtime TerminologyProvider — patient / client / member / consumer / resident by setting. Surface that control in the documentation chrome itself, next to framework and density. A behavioral-health reader who flips the site to “client” and watches every example follow has learned more about your domain understanding than any paragraph could tell them.

Why /patterns is the highest-value addition

A component library answers “how do I render an allergy?”. A healthcare team asks “how should a critical result get acknowledged?” — and that question has no page anywhere on the internet worth reading. A pattern page is a composed workflow with the safety reasoning attached, and it does four jobs at once: it captures long-tail search that no competitor contests, it gives the clinical reader a page they recognise, it gives an executive something to forward, and it makes the components legible as a system rather than a bag of parts.

Eight to launch, each named as the clinician’s job rather than the component’s name:

Acknowledging a critical result Showing a record you may not fully display Reviewing what a model wrote Recording a refusal Handover and coverage Running a scored assessment A medication with a monitoring schedule Reconciling an amended result

URL rules that must not be broken later

  • Components stay flat at /components/[name]. Categories change; URLs must not. A component with three legitimate parents cannot live under one of them.
  • Hubs are nested, pages are not. /components/behavioral-health is a curated index that links to flat pages.
  • Anchors look like tabs, never unmount. Real tabs break find-in-page, deep links and crawlers. This is already the shipped approach — keep it and write it down.
  • Every page has a canonical, a description under 160 characters, and no orphan. Enforce all three in the docs quality gate (§18), not in review.

Navigation

Recommended primary nav Components · Patterns · Architecture · Enterprise — with search (⌘K) and a persistent Talk to an engineer. Drop Sign in / Sign up from the primary bar entirely; move account entry to the footer and to the point of need inside /marketplace. Nothing about a login belongs in the first impression of a product whose core is free.

Documentation as product

Documentation architecture

The central claim of this section: a component page is a projection of a schema, not a document. Everything about scaling to hundreds of components without drift follows from that one decision, and roughly half the machinery already exists in the repository.

What already ships packages/component-meta has defineComponentMeta() with Zod validation. scripts/gen/ emits six artefacts — registry, catalog, tsconfig paths, Tailwind sources, llms.txt, coverage — and pnpm gen --check fails CI on staleness. Props are already extracted from TypeScript. This is not a system to build; it is a system to extend.

The fifteen regions

Fixed order, every component, no exceptions. Three of the orderings are deliberate departures from the obvious arrangement and each is worth defending:

  • “When to use it” sits above Usage — it is the passage an AI answer engine quotes, and the paragraph that saves an engineer from choosing the wrong component.
  • Accessibility sits above Related — because Related is a departure point, and departure points go last.
  • Clinical is one discrete region, conditioned on domain.industries, so a non-clinical primitive is structurally free of it rather than carrying an empty heading.

Two decisions I would take now

Sandbox
No “open in CodeSandbox” button. It cannot resolve your registry, tokens or fixtures, so it opens broken — and a broken sandbox does more damage than a missing one. Ship a first-party playground on the page instead; the schema already knows the controls.
Source visibility
Show every component’s source, including Pro; gate the install only. Hiding source destroys the SEO and trust value that is the entire reason this catalogue works, and it protects nothing — the value is in maintenance and support, not in secrecy.
10
The moat

Healthcare differentiation strategy

This is the section that decides whether Oxygen UI is a component library with healthcare labels or something a general-purpose library cannot become. The good news is that the answer is already engineered — it is simply not presented as a strategy.

Five things that make a system healthcare-native

Each of these is a property of the contract, not of the styling. That is the test: if a competitor could reach parity by restyling, it is not differentiation.

01

Absence is a typed state with a reason

Not asked · not interpreted · restricted · pending · unusable. Five different facts that a generic library renders as one em-dash. {value ?? "—"} is a lint error.

02

Meaning is never inferred

A stated interpretation always wins; otherwise the direction comes only from the observation’s own reference range. Never a population default, never a range from another specimen or age band.

03

Severity is never colour alone

Hue, glyph and word are emitted together or not at all, with 60° of hue separation and a validated contrast floor — so direction survives colour-vision deficiency and forced-colors mode.

04

Visibility is one policy, evaluated once

The Access & Disclosure Kernel: masking, break-glass, Part 2 segmentation, minor consent and role scope behind a single evaluation, so six components cannot disagree about what a user may see.

05

Provenance travels with the value

Where it came from and how much of it a human has actually read — carried through to the DOM, so origin cannot be lost by a re-render or a copy-paste.

The test

Could a general-purpose library ship this by restyling?

No — every one of the five is a change to what a prop means. That is why this is a moat and a nicer-looking card is not.

The behavioral-health bet

The phase-two plan makes 11 of 50 components behavioral-health-specific. That is a bet that the buyer is a behavioral-health platform; if the buyer is general digital health, the right list is about six. The website does not have to wait for that answer. Component-page refinements

Make one claim per page falsifiable in the browser

The rule most at risk of being lost A 10.5 px uppercase micro-label was once filed under the 3:1 large-text floor when it is small text owing 4.5:1. Fixing it honestly compressed the ink ramp, because the contrast floor genuinely limits how many greys can carry small text on a light ground. The consequence: four text steps is what WCAG allows, and the fifth grey is a non-text token that may not carry a word. That is written in a CSS comment today. It belongs in the token package's README, because the next person to add a grey will not read that comment.

The measurement worth running before and after

When this repository last audited its own mockups the numbers were: 1,985 inline styles, 2,916 literal values, 802 raw hexes, 52 distinct paddings, 22 font sizes, 0 transitions, 1 :focus-visible rule. After a token pass: literals down 80%, token references from 2 to 2,340, hexes from 802 to 25. Run the same count against apps/docs. It is a cheap, objective check on whether the site is built from the system it is advertising — and it will find things a design review will not.

One fix, then leave it alone

Motion and interaction

The motion system is already good and already principled — the CSS says so in its own comment: “nothing loops decoratively on top of clinical content — that is the exact behaviour the component tokens prohibit, and the site has to hold itself to it.” One thing is wrong with it, and it is serious.

The fix Scroll reveal must be a transform, never opacity from zero. Today [data-reveal] sets opacity: 0 until an IntersectionObserver fires — 47 elements on the homepage. When the observer does not run, the page is blank below the fold. It may set translateY(14px) and settle to zero; it may not set opacity. That single change is invisible to a sighted reader on a working page, and it makes the homepage exist for crawlers, for print, and for anyone whose JavaScript failed.

What to keep exactly as it is

  • The two curves. --ease-out-expo for surfaces and --ease-spring reserved for the few places overshoot is wanted. Two named curves with distinct jobs is the right number.
  • The hero entrance plays on load, not on scroll. The comment explaining why — “above-the-fold content should never wait for an observer” — is exactly right, and is the same reasoning the reveal fix applies to everything else.
  • The telemetry trace as the single ambient element. One ambient animation on the page, and it is the one thing that genuinely represents time passing.
  • The loaders. Paced to resting physiology, clamped below 1.7 Hz, with a designed still state under prefers-reduced-motion. This is a genuine, defensible asset that nobody else has a reason for. It deserves more prominence in the content (§7) and no change at all in the code.

Two small additions

Copy confirmation
The install line's copy control should swap to a checkmark for about 1.2 s and announce “copied” to assistive technology. It is the most-used control on the site.
Reduced motion, stated
The components treat prefers-reduced-motion as a designed still state rather than an absence. The site should say so on the page, because it is a differentiator that currently only exists in the code.

Turning readers into clients

Enterprise conversion strategy

The conversion problem is not that the current site converts badly. It is that it has no conversion mechanism at all — no offer, no route, no contact. Everything below is additive.

The mechanism: an offer, not a call-to-action

“Contact us” converts nobody. A specific, bounded, obviously valuable offer converts people who were already half-persuaded, which is exactly who arrives at the bottom of a page like this.

The offer

A 45-minute technical review of one clinical screen. You send a screenshot or a URL. An engineer — the one who would run the engagement, not a salesperson — comes back with what would break, what is unsafe, and what we would do first.

It is credible because it is small, it qualifies ruthlessly because only someone with a real screen can take it up, and it demonstrates the product in the act of selling it.

Where the conversion surfaces live

The rule is that conversion appears at the point where competence has just been demonstrated, never before it and never as an interruption.

  • End of a pattern page: the highest-intent moment on the site. Someone who has read “acknowledging a critical result” to the end has a workflow problem. One line: “We build these. Send us the screen you are stuck on.”
  • Inside #accessibility: a reader in that section is often there because procurement asked. That is the audit lead, and it arrives pre-qualified.
  • Inside #quality: after the CI evidence — “this is the standard we hold client work to as well.”
  • Never: a modal, an exit-intent overlay, a newsletter interstitial, or a gate in front of source. In this market those cost more trust than they capture leads.

Held to the same bar

Accessibility strategy

The components are tested to WCAG 2.2 AA across three engines. The website is not held to the same standard, and that gap is both a credibility risk and the easiest thing in this report to fix — the harness already exists.

The principle

Rule The site is subject to the component test suite. Same axe run, same three engines, same 320 px reflow and 200 % zoom checks, same forced-colors pass. A marketing page that fails the standard its own components are gated on is the most expensive kind of inconsistency, because the people who notice are exactly the people you are trying to convert.

Six requirements specific to this site

  • Content before observers. Text is present and selectable before any IntersectionObserver runs. This is defect D3 and it is an accessibility defect before it is an SEO one.
  • The dual-read control is a real control. A labelled switch with aria-pressed, reachable by keyboard, announcing which reading is active. Not a styled div.
  • State chips are a radio group, arrow-key navigable, with the selection announced — and the demo’s change announced in a live region, because a silent swap is invisible to a screen reader.
  • Code blocks are focusable and scrollable by keyboard. A horizontally scrolling <pre> that cannot be reached by keyboard is a 2.1.1 failure and is extremely common on documentation sites.
  • Forced-colors mode is designed, not survived. Every status carries a glyph and a word, so the clinical meaning holds when the palette is replaced. This is already true of the components; the site’s own chrome must match.
  • Focus is visible on every interactive element, with a 2 px offset outline that meets the non-text contrast floor against both grounds.

Publish the evidence

Almost nobody does this, and it converts. On every component page, in the accessibility region: the keyboard map, the roles emitted, the accessibility tree the component actually produces, and the name of the test that asserts each claim. A claim with a test name beside it reads differently from a claim without one — and it turns your accessibility work into an audit lead (§17) rather than a footnote.

Two claims from the repository are worth featuring by name, because they show the difference between testing rendering and testing safety:

Level A, as a test not a sentence

One test signs the form using tab() and keyboard() only, never dispatching a pointer event. The corresponding authoring mistake — a draw-only signature pad — is a lint error, because it renders perfectly and passes every other test.

expectStatedInWords asserts a state is readable without colour. It is the assertion that makes “never colour alone” a gate rather than a guideline.

A documentation site’s performance problem is almost never the framework. It is shipping every demo on every page, and shipping fonts and syntax highlighting the reader has not asked for yet.

Five decisions that do the work

  1. Demos hydrate on visibility, content does not wait for it. The static render is the real component’s markup; hydration attaches interactivity. A reader who never scrolls to the playground never pays for it — and a crawler that never hydrates still sees the component.
  2. Syntax highlighting happens at build. Ship HTML with classes, not a highlighter. This is typically the single largest saving on a docs site.
  3. The catalogue page virtualises above roughly 60 cards. At 26 it is unnecessary; at the planned 76 it is not. Build it before it is needed, because retrofitting virtualisation into a page with per-card live art is unpleasant.
  4. Card art is static at rest. The live component art is a real differentiator and it should animate on hover or focus only — twenty-six simultaneously animating loaders is a fan-spin, not a demonstration.
  5. The telemetry strip is build-time data. No request, no skeleton, no layout shift.
Measure the right page Do not benchmark the homepage. Benchmark /components/care-timeline — the heaviest page in the catalogue, with the most demos and the largest component. If the budget holds there it holds everywhere, and it is the page a sceptical evaluator will open on a train.

How it gets built

Implementation architecture

The migration is unusually low-risk because the content model is already generated. The work is mostly a new page template, three new content types, and the discipline to keep pages generated.

The order that de-risks it

  1. Extend the schema first. Add the new fields to defineComponentMeta() with everything optional, and land the Zod validation. Nothing visible changes; the generator keeps passing. This is the foundation and it can ship in isolation.
  2. Build the page template against one component. ResultValue is the right subject — it is stable, it has the most states, and it exercises every region including the conditional one. Run the new template alongside the existing page at a preview route.
  3. Flip the catalogue. Once one page is right, the other 25 are a loop, and the gates in §18 tell you which ones are incomplete rather than requiring a manual audit.
  4. Then the marketing surfaces. Homepage, patterns and enterprise last — they depend on the component pages for their live demos, and building them first would mean building the demos twice.
Why this order and not the obvious one The instinct is to redesign the homepage first because it is the most visible. That inverts the dependency: the homepage’s workflow section, its dual read and its catalogue strip all render the same component demos the documentation needs. Build the parts once, at the bottom, and the top assembles itself.

The per-component recipe is already long — do not lengthen it

Adding a component currently touches seven places: the registry lib and its support item, path aliases in two files, the stylesheet in three places, component tokens, preview scenarios and card art, and reciprocal related edges. Every one of those is guarded by a test, which is why it works — but it is at the limit of what a contributor will get right.

Every new documentation field must be derived, not hand-authored, or the recipe grows and the discipline that holds all of this together breaks at exactly the moment the catalogue gets big enough to matter. If a field cannot be derived, it should be optional and its absence should degrade the page gracefully rather than fail it.

What I would do

Recommended direction

Keep the design. Fix the site. Below: why that is the right call, the sequence I would run it in, and an honest record of how this recommendation arrived here.

Why keep it

Keep the current visual system and spend the budget on what is missing from it. I reached that by trying the alternative: five directions in one register, then five in another, then five built to enterprise reference tiers. None of them beat what ships today, and the exercise was worth running because it produced the reason why.

The current design is doing three things that are expensive to buy and easy to lose. It is light-first, which is what clinical software actually looks like. Its hero is a composed object rather than a headline beside a screenshot — layered cards at different elevations forming a live instrument panel, which is the hardest single element on the site to replace. And it carries real typographic and token discipline: axis labels at 0.18em tracking, tabular numerals throughout, a two-tone focus ring engineered for both grounds, four themes including a reachable high-contrast at a 7:1 floor.

Against that, every serious problem I found is a problem of content, completeness or conversion. The placeholder where the argument should be. Three headline numbers that render as zero without JavaScript. A homepage where 52% of the height is a component catalogue that already has its own page. No team, no services and no contact anywhere. None of those is fixed by a new stylesheet, and all of them are fixed by the eleven items in §11.

The general principle, since it will come up again A redesign is the right answer when the design is preventing the business from working. Here the design is the most finished part of the product and the business surfaces are the unfinished part. Rebuilding the finished thing would have been the most expensive way to avoid the actual work.

How this recommendation changed

This report changed its recommendation twice. Recording that plainly, because the reasoning is more useful than the conclusion.

  1. First pass — five directions, rejected as generic. They shared one layout, one hero object and, in four of five cases, one teal accent. The diagnosis that mattered came from reading the repository afterwards: the live site already uses Instrument Sans, JetBrains Mono, an .instrument bezel and --color-trace: #2dd4b0. I had proposed the client's own site back to them, less well executed.
  2. Second pass — five concept-led directions, rejected as too fancy. An anaesthesia record, a radiology viewbox, a control-room console, an ISOTYPE plate. Distinctive, and wrong for an enterprise product site. That was art direction solving a problem the brief did not have.
  3. Third pass — five enterprise reference tiers. Credible, and still worse than what ships, because the shipping design already occupies the best of those tiers and adds a healthcare-specific hero object none of them had.
  4. The conclusion. Three attempts to beat the current design is enough evidence that it should be kept. The remaining twenty sections of this report — research, positioning, information architecture, documentation architecture, SEO, accessibility, performance — were never about the visual language and all still stand.
